# Abstract

Across the 10,11M km² of Eastern and Pacific Siberia, early settlement
clustered at productive salmon, seal and river-crossing sites such as
Pleistocene Ushki I-V in Kamchatka (45 residents in 10000BC)
[\[1\]](#ref-Goebel2003), High Arctic Zhokhov (38 residents in 6000BC)
[\[2\]](#ref-SikoraPitulko2019), Early Neolithic Kitoi camps at Shamanka
II and Lokomotiv on Lake Baikal [\[3\]](#ref-Weber2016a) and the large
Neolithic pit-house village on Suchu Island in the Lower Amur (185
residents in 3000BC) [\[4\]](#ref-Filatova2018). During the Bronze Age,
Early Iron Age and Middle Ages, permanent winter villages and walled
towns (*gorodishcha*) emerged across the southern steppe and monsoon
valleys: Karasuk Torgazhak (150 residents in 1000BC)
[\[5\]](#ref-Legrand2006), Xiongnu Ivolga Fortress in Transbaikalia
(2,8k residents in 100BC) [\[6\]](#ref-Vasyutin2022), the Uyghur
fortresses of Por-Bajin and Shagonar in Tuva (800AD)
[\[7\]](#ref-Tulush2016), the Bohai port of Kraskinskoye (2,2k in 800AD)
[\[8\]](#ref-Gelman2018) and the Jurchen Jin and Eastern Xia mountain
cities of Krasnoyarovskoye (15k in 1220AD) and Shayginskoye (3,8k) in
Primorye [\[9\]](#ref-Artemyeva2013)[\[10\]](#ref-Artemyeva2025),
alongside Bering Strait whaling villages at Ekven and Uelen (250-300
residents) [\[11\]](#ref-Dneprovskiy2017) and Sakha *alaas* settlements
at Sayylyk and Ayyy-Taybyt [\[12\]](#ref-Kiryanov2019). From the 17th
century, Russian river forts (*ostrogi*) founded at Yeniseysk (1619),
Krasnoyarsk (1628), Yakutsk (1632), Irkutsk (1661), Nerchinsk (1658) and
Petropavlovsk (1740), followed after 1858 by the Far Eastern ports and
railway cities, grew into an urban network led in 1897-1900AD by Irkutsk
(51,5k in 1897), Blagoveshchensk (32,8k), Vladivostok (28,9k),
Krasnoyarsk (26,7k), Khabarovsk (15k), Chita (11,5k) and Yeniseysk
(11,5k)
[\[13\]](#ref-Kabuzan1985)[\[14\]](#ref-Troinitskiy1905)[\[15\]](#ref-Patkanov1912).

# Methodology

This gazetteer presents the coordinates, native and historical names,
dates of occupation and population estimates for 231 settlements across
Eastern and Pacific Siberia from 10000BC to 1900AD (Figure ; Tables to
). Occupation spans correspond to the earliest and latest benchmark
dates recorded for each settlement.

Prehistoric camp and pit-house village figures are derived from
excavated house counts, floor areas and cemetery sizes
[\[3\]](#ref-Weber2016a)[\[11\]](#ref-Dneprovskiy2017)[\[16\]](#ref-Chernykh2022)[\[17\]](#ref-Kuzmin2006)[\[18\]](#ref-AlekseyevDyakonov2009)[\[19\]](#ref-Fitzhugh2016).
Estimates for Iron Age and medieval fortified settlements
(*gorodishcha*) in Transbaikalia, Tuva, Khakassia, the Amur Basin and
Primorye are drawn from walled areas and excavated house-pit totals
[\[6\]](#ref-Vasyutin2022)[\[7\]](#ref-Tulush2016)[\[8\]](#ref-Gelman2018)[\[9\]](#ref-Artemyeva2013)[\[10\]](#ref-Artemyeva2025)[\[20\]](#ref-Tulush2011)[\[21\]](#ref-Ivliev2021).
Early Russian forts (*ostrogi*), 18th-century mining and border towns
and 19th-century towns are compiled from *yasak* and garrison rolls
[\[22\]](#ref-Dolgikh1960)[\[23\]](#ref-Chertykov2009)[\[24\]](#ref-Ushnitskiy2017a)[\[25\]](#ref-Turaev2013),
poll-tax revision lists and urban histories
[\[13\]](#ref-Kabuzan1985)[\[26\]](#ref-Mansheev2010)[\[27\]](#ref-Kurchatova2015)[\[28\]](#ref-Kyshpanakov2022a)[\[29\]](#ref-Kolomiets2022)[\[30\]](#ref-Rygalova2024)
and the First All-Russian Census of 1897
[\[14\]](#ref-Troinitskiy1905)[\[15\]](#ref-Patkanov1912).

settlements in Chukotka, the Anadyr basin and the Bering Strait,
contrasting Old Bering Sea and Punuk whaling villages (Ekven, Uelen,
Sireniki) with the Russian outposts of Anadyrsk, Markovo and Gizhiginsk.
