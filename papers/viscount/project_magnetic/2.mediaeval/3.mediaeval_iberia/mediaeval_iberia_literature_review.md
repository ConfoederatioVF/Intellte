# Abstract

Between the Umayyad landing at Gibraltar in 711AD and the capitulation
of Nasrid Granada in 1492AD, the population of Iberia and its associated
Mediterranean and Atlantic archipelagos grew by 79,88%, rising from 5M
to 8,98M across 605.038,2 km² after reaching a pre-Black Death peak of
9,2M in 1347AD. Mean regional density climbed from 8,4 inhabitants per
km² in 711AD to 15,46 per km² on the eve of the mid-fourteenth-century
plague pandemic and stood at 15,11 per km² in 1492AD, yielding a
long-run compound growth rate of 0,075% per annum. Beneath that gradual
expansion lay a decisive north-south demographic inversion. At the
zenith of the Umayyad Caliphate of Cordoba in 1000AD, Al-Andalus held
40,74% of peninsular inhabitants (2,65M at 25,3 per km²) and six of
Iberia’s ten largest cities, led by Cordoba (100k) and Sevilla (72k).
Over the ensuing five centuries, Christian Reconquista colonisation,
Castilian Meseta expansion, recurrent plague mortality and the
fragmentation of Islamic polities reversed that balance: by 1492AD the
Crown of Castile-Leon held 43,96% of the total population (3,95M),
Al-Andalus had receded to 23,93% (2,15M) and urban primacy had shifted
to maritime Valencia (71,3k) and Lisboa (64,1k). MAGNETIC synthesises
fiscal hearth counts, archival city series, bioarchaeological and
archaeobotanical indicators and a georeferenced gazetteer of 8.963
settlements across five sub-regions to reconstruct these long-run
trajectories.

# Background

In 711AD the Iberian Peninsula was a sparsely settled post-Roman
landscape whose late Visigothic population had barely recovered from
sixth-century Justinianic plague recurrences, civil wars and
intermittent harvest failures
[\[1\]](#ref-Russell1958)[\[2\]](#ref-Martinpuertas2010Late). Roughly 5M
people occupied 605.038,2 km² at an average density of 8,4 inhabitants
per km². Settlement remained heavily tilted towards the old Roman
*Baetica* and the Mediterranean littoral, where alluvial valleys, olive
groves and surviving municipal nuclei still sustained densities
approaching 19 inhabitants per km², whereas the high interior Meseta and
the rainy northern mountain rim carried scarcely 5 to 7 people per km².
When Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the Strait of Gibraltar in spring 711AD and
defeated King Roderic near the Guadalete, the Visigothic state collapsed
within five years. Yet the Arab and Berber forces that entered the
peninsula numbered only in the tens of thousands; they formed a military
and fiscal elite superimposed upon an overwhelmingly Hispano-Roman rural
majority whose gradual conversion to Islam (*muwalladun*) and
Arabisation shaped Andalusi society over the next three centuries
[\[3\]](#ref-Macroberts2024Shrouded).

The establishment of the independent Umayyad Emirate at Cordoba by Abd
al-Rahman I in 756AD and the proclamation of the Caliphate by Abd
al-Rahman III in 929AD coincided with one of the sharpest urban and
agricultural expansions in early mediaeval Europe. Benefiting from the
warmer, more stable precipitation patterns of the Mediaeval Climate
Anomaly across the southwestern Mediterranean
[\[2\]](#ref-Martinpuertas2010Late), Andalusi cultivators extended and
rehabilitated hydraulic irrigation networks (*acequias*, *qanats* and
animal-driven *norias*) throughout the Guadalquivir, Genil, Segura and
Turia basins. New summer crops diffused from the eastern Islamic world —
hard wheat, rice, cotton, sugarcane, citrus, aubergine and white
mulberry for sericulture — permitted double cropping on irrigated
*huertas* and raised calorie yields per hectare far above dryland Roman
norms, paralleling contemporary dietary and ceramic transformations
observed in Islamic Sicily [\[4\]](#ref-Lundy2021Insights).

Bioarchaeological evidence from eighth-to-tenth-century Santarém along
the lower Tagus shows a well-integrated urban-frontier Muslim community
maintaining mixed cereal, legume and caprine diets under Umayyad rule
[\[3\]](#ref-Macroberts2024Shrouded), while glass assemblages from
Ciudad de Vascos near Toledo attest to long-distance Mediterranean
exchange alongside vigorous local craft production inside fortified
provincial *madinas* [\[5\]](#ref-Ares2017Glass). Between 711AD and
1000AD the population of Al-Andalus expanded by 35,9% from 1,95M to
2,65M, lifting the Iberian aggregate from 5M to 6,5M. Cordoba reached
100k inhabitants, rivalled in Europe only by Constantinople, while
Sevilla (72k), Toledo (37k), Merida (30k), Jaen (20k), Jerez de la
Frontera (19k), Ecija (18k) and the naval port of Almeria (18k) anchored
a dense network of monetised markets, madrasas and artisanal quarters
[\[6\]](#ref-Bairoch1988)[\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021)[\[8\]](#ref-Qureshi2017Mystics).

Political unity in the south fractured abruptly after 1009AD. The
Cordoban *fitna* sacked Madinat al-Zahra, dismantled the Caliphate by
1031AD and splintered Al-Andalus into more than two dozen competing
*Taifa* kingdoms. Urban prosperity did not collapse everywhere
immediately — Sevilla, Granada, Zaragoza, Badajoz and Valencia continued
to expand as regional courts — yet tributary extortion (*parias*) paid
to the northern Christian kings drained Andalusi silver and destabilised
rural taxation. The intervention of North African empires, first the
Almoravids after 1086AD and then the Almohads after 1147AD, temporarily
halted Christian advances and elevated Sevilla into the peninsula’s
foremost Islamic metropolis, reaching 80k inhabitants by 1200AD.
Nevertheless, intermittent frontier raids, deportations of Mozarab
communities and the gradual loss of the Tagus and Ebro valleys eroded
the demographic base of southern Al-Andalus, whose population eased from
2,65M in 1000AD to 2,45M in 1200AD.

North of the Duero and Ebro rivers, a contrasting demographic engine was
gathering speed. In the eighth and ninth centuries the small Christian
realms of Asturias, Pamplona-Navarre and the Catalan counties of the
Carolingian Hispanic March held cramped mountain valleys and thinly
populated border marches. From the late ninth century onwards, monarchs,
monasteries and secular magnates organised systematic frontier
colonisation (*repoblación*), first through free peasant *presura*
grants across the Duero plains and later through chartered municipal
*fueros* along the Trans-Duero and Tagus corridors. Castle-centred
lordships in interior Galicia, exemplified by sites such as San Salvador
de Todea in Ourense, consolidated rural surplus extraction and local
defence across dissected northern uplands [\[9\]](#ref-Novoa2022Origen).
Archaeobotanical records demonstrate that the mediaeval expansion of rye
(*Secale cereale*) played a decisive agronomic role across the cool,
acidic soils of northwestern Iberia, northern Portugal and the northern
Meseta, allowing cultivators to colonise higher elevations and shorter
growing seasons where wheat failed
[\[10\]](#ref-Seabra2023Introduction). The capture of Toledo (1085AD),
Zaragoza (1118AD), Lisboa (1147AD) and Tortosa (1148AD) opened immense
fertile plains to Christian settlers, while the crushing victory over
the Almohads at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212AD broke the military barrier
of the Sierra Morena. Within four decades James I of Aragon conquered
Mallorca (1229AD) and Valencia (1238AD), Ferdinand III of Castile took
Cordoba (1236AD), Jaen (1246AD) and Sevilla (1248AD), and Afonso III of
Portugal completed the occupation of the Algarve (1249AD).

Territorial conquest outran northern manpower. Across western Andalusia,
Extremadura and La Mancha, the expulsion or flight of much of the Muslim
peasantry after the 1264AD Mudejar revolt left extensive royal and noble
*repartimientos* chronically short of labour. Landowners and military
orders responded by converting abandoned arable into vast estates
(*latifundia*) and seasonal pastures; in 1273AD Alfonso X incorporated
the *Honrado Concejo de la Mesta*, institutionalising long-distance
transhumance of millions of merino sheep between Cantabrian-Leonese
summer ranges and Extremaduran-Andalusian winter grazing grounds. By
contrast, in the newly conquered Kingdom of Valencia and the Ebro
valley, Aragonese and Catalan lords retained dense rural Mudejar
workforces on irrigated terraces, sustaining high agricultural output
alongside booming coastal commerce in Barcelona and Palma. Meanwhile,
refugees fleeing conquered Andalusia crowded into the mountain-ringed
Nasrid Emirate of Granada (established 1238AD), inflating the city of
Granada from 20k in 1200AD to 50k by 1300AD and giving the diminutive
emirate one of the highest rural densities in western Europe. Overall
Iberian population rose from 8M in 1200AD to 9,2M in 1347AD, a level not
surpassed again until the sixteenth century.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries subjected this agrarian order to
severe ecological, epidemic and political shocks. Cooling and heightened
hydroclimatic variability signalling the early onset of the Little Ice
Age destabilised harvests from the 1310s onwards, with acute subsistence
crises hitting Castile, Catalonia and Navarre in 1333AD (*lo mal any
primer*) and 1343-1347AD
[\[2\]](#ref-Martinpuertas2010Late)[\[11\]](#ref-Campbell2016). In the
spring of 1348AD Genoese and Catalan shipping carried *Yersinia pestis*
directly into Palma de Mallorca, Barcelona, Valencia and Sevilla, from
which the Black Death spread rapidly across the interior Meseta,
Portugal and Nasrid Granada [\[12\]](#ref-Benedictow2004). Mortality
varied sharply by ecology and connectivity: coastal Catalonia, Mallorca
and the crowded quarters of Aragon suffered catastrophic losses
exceeding 35% to 45%, whereas parts of northern Castile and interior
highlands experienced somewhat lighter mortality and swifter post-plague
rebound, a spatial heterogeneity mirrored across continental
palaeoecological pollen archives [\[13\]](#ref-Izdebski2022).

Recurrent plague waves in 1362-1363AD, 1371-1375AD, 1384AD and the 1410s
depressed Mediterranean Iberia for over a century. In Barcelona,
pestilence compounded banking collapses, the anti-Jewish pogroms of
1391AD that destroyed the city’s historic *call*
[\[14\]](#ref-Martinezarino2020Jewish) and the Catalan Civil War of
1462-1472AD, halving the city from 48k in 1300AD to 21,1k in 1492AD.
Across Castile, however, demographic recovery accelerated sharply after
1420AD as rural birth rates rebounded, wool exports enriched Meseta fair
towns such as Medina del Campo, Valladolid and Burgos, and Sevilla
prepared for Atlantic trade. Along the western seaboard, Portugal
consolidated independence after Aljubarrota (1385AD), expanded Lisboa
into a 64,1k maritime entrepôt and colonised uninhabited Madeira
(1419AD) and the Azores (1430s), while Castilian expeditions gradually
conquered the Canary Islands (completing Gran Canaria in 1483AD and
initiating the final campaigns on La Palma and Tenerife in 1492-1496AD).
When Ferdinand and Isabella entered Granada on 2 January 1492AD and
promulgated the Alhambra Decree expelling the Sephardic Jewish
communities three months later, Iberia stood at 8,98M inhabitants —
nearly matching its 1347AD ceiling, yet fundamentally reorganised around
Castile, Lisboa and Valencia.

# Methodology

Demographic reconstruction for mediaeval Iberia confronts an asymmetric
archival record. Direct territory-wide censuses do not exist for the
Umayyad Caliphate, the Taifa states or the early Christian kingdoms
prior to the mid-fourteenth century. Rather than extrapolating
mechanically from any single modern back-projection, MAGNETIC combines
five independent evidence streams across five fixed areal sub-regions
totalling 605.038,2 km²: (1) late-mediaeval fiscal hearth enumerations
(*fogatges* in Catalonia, Valencia and Mallorca from 1359AD, 1365AD,
1378AD and 1497AD; *vecindarios* and royal *pechero* assessments in
Castile from 1482AD and the early sixteenth century; and the 1527-1532AD
Portuguese *Numeramento* back-projected to 1492AD); (2) standardised
longitudinal urban population series for European and Mediterranean
cities [\[6\]](#ref-Bairoch1988)[\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021); (3) regional
rural density benchmarks derived from comparative European agrarian
demography
[\[1\]](#ref-Russell1958)[\[15\]](#ref-Malanima1998)[\[16\]](#ref-Broadberry2015)[\[17\]](#ref-Guzowski2013);
(4) environmental and archaeological proxies tracking land clearance,
crop diffusion, settlement continuity and post-plague abandonment
[\[2\]](#ref-Martinpuertas2010Late)[\[3\]](#ref-Macroberts2024Shrouded)[\[9\]](#ref-Novoa2022Origen)[\[10\]](#ref-Seabra2023Introduction)[\[13\]](#ref-Izdebski2022);
and (5) a spatially explicit gazetteer of 8.963 habitation localities.

The five sub-regional polygons partition the Iberian macro-domain along
durable political-ecological boundaries rather than shifting frontier
lines so that longitudinal populations and densities remain strictly
comparable across all five benchmark dates (711AD, 1000AD, 1200AD,
1347AD and 1492AD). First, Al-Andalus, Andalusia and Granada (118.761
km²) encompasses the Guadalquivir basin, the Baetic cordilleras,
Murcia-Cartagena and the historic core of the Caliphate and Nasrid
Emirate. Second, the Crown of Castile-Leon (269.919,4 km²) spans Old and
New Castile, Leon, Galicia, Asturias, Cantabria, the Basque provinces,
Extremadura and Navarre, accounting for 49,01% of total domain area.
Third, the Crown of Aragon, Catalonia and Valencia (123.550,2 km²)
covers the Ebro depression, Pyrenean valleys, Catalan littoral and
Valencian *huerta*. Fourth, the Kingdom of Portugal and Atlantic Isles
(85.325,6 km²) pairs mainland Portugal with the fifteenth-century
Macaronesian colonies of Madeira and the Azores. Fifth, the Balearic and
Canary Islands (7.482 km²) groups the western Mediterranean Balearic
archipelago with the Castilian Canary archipelago.

Converting mediaeval fiscal registers into total headcount requires
explicit treatment of household multipliers and tax-exempt strata. In
Catalan and Valencian *fogatges* (hearth counts), multipliers typically
fall between 4,1 and 4,5 persons per taxable hearth once unenumerated
poor households, clergy and floating urban labour are incorporated; in
late-fifteenth-century Castilian *vecindarios*, a coefficient near 4,3
to 4,5 persons per *vecino* reconciles hearth lists with
early-sixteenth-century parish evidence and comparative Mediterranean
urban household ratios
[\[1\]](#ref-Russell1958)[\[15\]](#ref-Malanima1998). For pre-1300AD
benchmarks, where fiscal hearths are absent or fragmentary, sub-regional
aggregates are anchored to two constraints: minimum agro-ecological
carrying capacities implied by irrigated versus dryland cereal systems
and archaeobotanical crop repertoires
[\[4\]](#ref-Lundy2021Insights)[\[10\]](#ref-Seabra2023Introduction),
and urban-to-rural scaling ratios calibrated against the longitudinal
city database [\[6\]](#ref-Bairoch1988)[\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021). Thus,
Cordoba’s 100k population and Sevilla’s 72k population in 1000AD require
an agricultural hinterland across southern Al-Andalus capable of
generating a grain, oil and livestock surplus compatible with an urban
share (cities of 10k or more) near 10% to 12% of the southern
sub-region, ruling out implausibly low rural densities below 15
inhabitants per km² while equally rejecting mediaeval chroniclers’
rhetorical claims of half a million residents in Cordoba alone.

The 8.963-locality habitation register was compiled through a four-wave
saturation protocol that prioritised institutional historical urban
databases and scholarly archaeological gazetteers before permitting any
municipal spatial supplement (Table 1). Wave 1 ingested 307 longitudinal
mediaeval city series from the Buringh European Urban Register
(700-2000) [\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021), capturing virtually every Iberian
town that reached 1k inhabitants between 750AD and 1492AD. Wave 2
incorporated 192 continuous Roman-to-mediaeval habitation localities
from the Pleiades Ancient World Gazetteer (a 62,54% marginal gain),
filling rural market centres, road stations and episcopal seats absent
from urban-threshold tables. Wave 3 audited supplementary municipal
backfill registries and added zero entries. Wave 4 conducted a
sub-regional coverage sweep across the Macaronesian archipelagos, adding
9 Canary Islands spatial anchors from the GeoNames Municipal Gazetteer
Supplement (a 1,8% marginal gain), while 85 out-of-domain or duplicate
candidates were excluded. Across the entire gazetteer, primary scholarly
corpora account for 98,23% of all included settlements, restricting
crowd-sourced supplementation to 1,77%.

# Validation

Four independent cross-checks assess the coherence of the reconstructed
macro-series and settlement hierarchy. First, the terminal 1492AD total
of 8,98M aligns closely with early-modern fiscal reconstructions of
Spain and Portugal at the opening of the sixteenth century. Subtracting
the Portuguese and Atlantic Isles component (1,22M) and the insular
Balearic-Canary share (185k) leaves 7,58M across mainland Spain (3,95M
in Castile-Leon and Navarre, 2,15M in Andalusia-Granada-Murcia and 1,48M
in Aragon-Catalonia-Valencia). That figure sits squarely within the
consensus band derived from the 1482AD Quintanilla assessment for
Castile (roughly 1,1M to 1,2M *vecinos*, or 5M to 5,5M inhabitants
including Andalusia and Murcia), the 1497AD Catalan *fogatge*
(approximately 59,9k hearths, or 270k to 290k residents after
post-civil-war recovery), Valencian and Aragonese hearth registers
(roughly 300k and 250k respectively) and the 1527-1532AD Portuguese
*Numeramento* (280k *fogos*, corresponding to 1,15M to 1,25M inhabitants
three decades earlier) [\[1\]](#ref-Russell1958).

Second, the 1347-1492AD inflection conforms to both epidemiological
historiography and continental palaeoecology. Iberia’s aggregate
population fell by only 2,28% net between 1347AD (9,2M) and 1492AD
(8,98M), yet that mild net change conceals a sharp fourteenth-century
trough followed by divergent fifteenth-century rebounds. Aggregate
recorded city population across the 508-settlement register dropped by
19,13% from 1,64M in 1300AD to 1,32M in 1400AD before climbing back to
1,72M by 1492AD — a classic V-shaped late-mediaeval demographic
trajectory matching England and Italy
[\[11\]](#ref-Campbell2016)[\[15\]](#ref-Malanima1998)[\[16\]](#ref-Broadberry2015).
Crucially, sub-regional divergence echoes recent European pollen
syntheses showing that Black Death mortality and agricultural
contraction were far less uniform than earlier uniform-loss models
assumed [\[12\]](#ref-Benedictow2004)[\[13\]](#ref-Izdebski2022).
Coastal Aragon-Catalonia-Valencia (-10,3% net across 1347-1492AD) and
Portugal (-9,63%) still sat well below their 1347AD peaks in 1492AD,
whereas interior Castile-Leon stood 8,22% above its pre-plague mark,
driven by rapid fifteenth-century rural resettlement and
textile-pastoral prosperity.

Third, urban-to-total ratios stay within realistic pre-industrial
Mediterranean bounds across every benchmark. Defining cities at the
standard 10k threshold
[\[6\]](#ref-Bairoch1988)[\[15\]](#ref-Malanima1998), recorded Iberian
urban residents totalled 450,8k across 18 cities in 1000AD (6,93% of the
6,51M peninsular total), 500,8k across 23 cities in 1200AD (6,26%),
554,7k across 22 cities in 1347AD (6,03%) and 640,2k across 29 cities in
1492AD (7,12%). Broadening the urban threshold to 5k inhabitants raises
the Iberian urbanisation ratio from 8,18% in 1000AD (31 towns, 531,8k)
and 9,1% in 1200AD (60 towns, 727,8k) to 11,09% in 1492AD (80 towns,
996,5k). These levels trail northern and central Italy’s 14% to 18%
late-mediaeval urbanisation [\[15\]](#ref-Malanima1998) yet consistently
outpace northern Europe and England’s 3% to 5% urban share
[\[16\]](#ref-Broadberry2015), faithfully capturing Iberia’s
intermediate Mediterranean position.

Fourth, the rank-size distribution of peak recorded populations across
all 508 settlements obeys an exceptionally tight power-law relationship
($\alpha = 0,78$, $R^2 = 0,99$). A Zipf exponent below unity (0,78) is
diagnostically expected for a politically divided peninsula lacking a
single primate capital: instead of one dominant metropolis, mediaeval
Iberia sustained multiple rival regional capitals — Cordoba, Sevilla,
Toledo, Leon, Barcelona, Valencia, Granada and Lisboa — across separate
linguistic and dynastic realms. Two spatial-data artefacts identified
during auditing do not distort macro-totals but require transparent
documentation: Palma de Mallorca (17k peak in 1300AD) falls inside the
Aragon-Catalonia mask polygon at the coastal boundary rather than the
Balearic-Canary polygon, and the 9 GeoNames Canary Islands anchors carry
flat static population tiers (including Telde at 14,8k) across
750-1492AD rather than historically dated Guanche-to-Castilian
trajectories.

# Results

## Peninsular Trajectory and Regional Rebalancing

Between 711AD and 1492AD the demographic trajectory of mediaeval Iberia
unfolded in four distinct intervals (Figure 1; Table 2). During the
Umayyad expansion from 711AD to 1000AD, total population rose by 30,23%
from 5M to 6,5M (0,091% per annum), led by Al-Andalus (+700k) and modest
gains across the northern Christian marches. Between 1000AD and 1200AD
growth accelerated to 0,103% per annum (+22,91%, adding 1,49M
inhabitants to reach 8M), powered entirely by the northern Christian
kingdoms as Castile-Leon added 1M inhabitants, Aragon-Catalonia 400k and
Portugal 260k while Al-Andalus contracted by 200k under civil strife and
frontier warfare. From 1200AD to 1347AD the pace remained vigorous at
0,095% per annum (+15,01%), lifting Iberia to its mediaeval ceiling of
9,2M across all five sub-regions even as Al-Andalus slipped another 100k
following the thirteenth-century *repartimientos*. Finally, between
1347AD and 1492AD the Black Death, recurrent plague cycles and political
upheavals produced a net contraction of 2,28% (-0,016% per annum) to
8,98M, masking a 300k net gain in Castile-Leon against combined losses
of 510k across the other four sub-regions.

The geographic redistribution of Iberian population across these eight
centuries is laid bare in Table 3. In 711AD southern Al-Andalus held
39,04% of the peninsula’s people on just 17,61% of its land; by 1000AD
its share reached 40,74% and its density of 25,3 inhabitants per km²
stood nearly four times higher than Castile-Leon’s 6,69 per km².
Thereafter the centre of demographic gravity marched northwards onto the
Meseta. Castile-Leon rose from 29,98% of the peninsular total in 1000AD
to 36,9% in 1200AD, 39,7% in 1347AD and 43,96% in 1492AD — more than
doubling its density from 5,32 to 13,55 inhabitants per km² over the
chapter span. Even at 1492AD, however, the southern Andalusi and insular
margins retained Iberia’s highest densities (20,53 and 24,73 inhabitants
per km² respectively), confirming the enduring ecological advantage of
irrigated Mediterranean lowlands over dryland continental plateaus.

## Al-Andalus, Andalusia and the Nasrid Emirate of Granada

Across 118.761 km², southern Iberia traced an asymmetric arc unique on
the European mainland: rapid early-mediaeval ascent followed by five
centuries of gradual contraction. Starting from 1,95M inhabitants in
711AD (18,62 per km²), Al-Andalus climbed to 2,65M in 1000AD (25,3 per
km²), retreated to 2,45M in 1200AD (23,4 per km²) and 2,35M in 1347AD
(22,44 per km²), and closed at 2,15M in 1492AD (20,53 per km²). Its 184
recorded localities make it the densest urban-settlement cluster in the
chapter (17,57 localities per 10k km²), split almost evenly between 95
longitudinal urban series from Buringh and 89 continuous
Roman-to-mediaeval settlements from Pleiades.

Caliphate urbanisation reached levels unmatched north of the Pyrenees
before the thirteenth century. Cordoba grew from 36,7k in 750AD to 75k
in 800AD, 95k in 900AD and 100k in 1000AD, supported by sprawling
artisanal suburbs (*arrabales*), leather and textile workshops, paper
mills and extensive royal estates along the Guadalquivir. Sevilla
maintained 72k inhabitants by 900-1000AD, while inland agricultural and
administrative centres — Jaen (20k), Jerez de la Frontera (19k) and
Ecija (18k) — alongside the Mediterranean ports of Almeria (18k) and
Cartagena (17k) completed a deeply integrated southern urban lattice
[\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021)[\[8\]](#ref-Qureshi2017Mystics). Following the
collapse of the Caliphate, Cordoba declined steadily to 80k in 1100AD,
60k in 1200-1300AD, 40k in 1400AD and 35,4k in 1492AD. Sevilla, elevated
to imperial capital under the Almohads and royal maritime base under
Castile after 1248AD, followed a later peak, climbing to 80k in 1200AD
and 90k in 1300AD before plague and demographic dislocation trimmed it
to 70k in 1400AD and 46,6k in 1492AD.

The demographic shock of the thirteenth-century Castilian conquest of
western Andalusia was severe. Large Muslim urban and rural communities
departed after the capitulations of Cordoba (1236AD), Sevilla (1248AD)
and the crushed Mudejar uprising of 1264-1266AD, leaving vast tracts of
the lower Guadalquivir underpopulated despite generous royal
*repartimiento* land grants. Cartagena plunged from 17k in 1200AD to 5k
in 1300AD and 3,9k in 1492AD, and Almeria fell from 18k in 1300AD to
9,2k in 1492AD. Conversely, those displaced Muslim artisans, farmers and
scholars poured into the Nasrid Emirate of Granada. Granada itself
surged from 11k in 1000AD and 20k in 1200AD to 50k in 1300AD, remaining
at 41,1k on the eve of the Black Death in 1347AD before plague and the
protracted War of Granada (1482-1492AD) reduced the city to 23,7k in
1492AD. Even so, the mountain-terraced Alpujarras, the Vega of Granada
and coastal Malaga sustained roughly 300k to 350k Nasrid subjects on
scarcely 30k km² right down to 1492AD, while upper Guadalquivir
Castilian frontier towns — Jaen (21,7k), Ubeda (18,9k), Ecija (16,3k)
and Baeza (13,5k) — rebounded vigorously during the late fifteenth
century.

## Crown of Castile-Leon and the Meseta Expansion

Covering 269.919,4 km² from the Galician rias and the Cantabrian
cordillera across the Duero and Tagus basins to Extremadura and La
Mancha, the Crown of Castile-Leon (incorporating Navarre within the
northern polygon) was the principal engine of mediaeval Iberian
demographic expansion. Total population rose more than
two-and-a-half-fold (+154,84%) from 1,55M in 711AD (5,32 per km²) to
1,95M in 1000AD (6,69 per km²), 2,95M in 1200AD (10,12 per km²), 3,65M
in 1347AD (12,52 per km²) and 3,95M in 1492AD (13,55 per km²).
Crucially, Castile-Leon was the sole sub-region in Iberia to stand
meaningfully larger in 1492AD than in 1347AD (+8,22%, adding 300k
inhabitants), confirming the vitality of its late-mediaeval rural and
commercial recovery.

Early mediaeval settlement in this vast interior was polarised between
the Muslim Middle March (*al-Thaghr al-Awsat*) along the Tagus and
Guadiana and the embryonic Christian kingdoms of the north. In
900-1000AD the two largest cities inside the polygon were still Islamic
frontier capitals: Merida peaked at 40k in 900AD (30k in 1000AD) before
declining into a modest Extremaduran town of 9,7k in 1492AD, Badajoz
reached 16k in 1000AD and Toledo rose from 25k in 800AD to 37k in
1000AD. Meanwhile, Christian *repoblación* south of the Cantabrian
watershed built an entirely new northern urban tier anchored by Leon
(rising from 5k in 800AD to 15k in 1000AD and peaking at 40k in 1200AD
as the seat of the Leonese monarchy) and Burgos (growing from 1k in
800AD to 18k in 1000AD and 27k in 1400AD as the hub of the Camino de
Santiago and Cantabrian wool trade). Along the Galician interior and
northern uplands, rye cultivation and castellar nucleation sustained
steady peasant expansion across terrain unsuited to Mediterranean wheat
[\[9\]](#ref-Novoa2022Origen)[\[10\]](#ref-Seabra2023Introduction).

After 1250AD the political union of Castile and Leon (1230AD) and the
opening of southern grazing ranges reconfigured the Meseta hierarchy.
Leon declined sharply once royal patronage shifted southwards, falling
from 40k in 1200AD to 17k in 1347AD and 5,3k in 1492AD, while Burgos
contracted from 27k in 1400AD to 8,8k in 1492AD as wool marketing
migrated towards the central Duero. In their place rose a vigorous
network of fifteenth-century Castilian administrative, textile and fair
cities: Toledo reached its mediaeval zenith of 45k in 1400AD (32,9k in
1492AD); Valladolid climbed steadily from 6k in 1000AD and 16k in 1200AD
to 29k in 1492AD as the favoured royal court and chancery seat;
Plasencia surged from 1k in 1200AD to 23k in 1492AD; Medina del Campo
reached 20,1k in 1492AD as Iberia’s premier international banking and
wool fair; Alcazar de San Juan grew from 3k in 1400AD to 17,2k in
1492AD; Medina de Rioseco reached 14,8k; Palencia climbed to 13,2k; and
the university city of Salamanca stood at 11,8k
[\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021). Along the Bay of Biscay, Basque ironworks and
maritime shipbuilding — centred on Bilbao and the coastal villas —
further diversified northern Castile’s pre-1492AD economy.

## Crown of Aragon, Catalonia and Valencia

Spanning 123.550,2 km² across the Ebro basin, the Pyrenees, Catalonia
and the Valencian coast, the Crown of Aragon nearly doubled (+97,33%)
between 711AD and 1492AD, rising from 750k (7,36 per km²) to 950k in
1000AD (9,32 per km²), 1,35M in 1200AD (13,24 per km²) and a pre-plague
peak of 1,65M in 1347AD (16,18 per km²), before falling 10,3% to 1,48M
in 1492AD (14,51 per km²). Its 87 recorded localities (52 from Buringh
and 35 from Pleiades) document one of the sharpest internal geographic
realignments in late-mediaeval Europe.

Between 1000AD and 1300AD Catalonia was the demographic and commercial
powerhouse of the eastern peninsula. Barcelona expanded nearly tenfold
from 5k in 1000AD to 23k in 1200AD and 48k in 1300AD, commanding
Mediterranean trade routes to Sicily, Sardinia, Alexandria and
Constantinople while Palma de Mallorca (assigned to the Aragon-Catalonia
coastal mask at 17k in 1300AD) served as its forward maritime staging
post. Inland, Zaragoza grew steadily from 5k in 1000AD to 11k in 1200AD
and 15k in 1300AD after Alfonso I’s 1118AD conquest integrated the
irrigated Ebro valley into the Aragonese crown.

From 1348AD onwards, however, Catalonia entered a century and a half of
severe demographic contraction. The Black Death struck Barcelona,
Girona, Tarragona and the Emporda with devastating intensity
[\[12\]](#ref-Benedictow2004), followed by recurring plagues, agrarian
rent struggles (*remensa* peasant revolts), the destruction of
Barcelona’s Jewish *call* in the 1391AD pogroms
[\[14\]](#ref-Martinezarino2020Jewish) and the ruinous Catalan Civil War
of 1462-1472AD. Barcelona shrank from 48k in 1300AD to 43k in 1347AD,
38k in 1400AD, 27,6k in 1450AD and just 21,1k in 1492AD — losing over
half its peak headcount. Over the very same period, the Kingdom of
Valencia experienced Iberia’s most dramatic late-mediaeval boom. Blessed
with intensive hydraulic irrigation tended by a large Mudejar rural
workforce, expanding silk and ceramic industries and direct access to
Italian and Castilian trade, the city of Valencia climbed relentlessly
from 8k in 1000AD and 18k in 1200AD to 27k in 1300AD, 32,5k in 1347AD,
40k in 1400AD, 54,8k in 1450AD and 71,3k in 1492AD, eclipsing Sevilla,
Cordoba and Lisboa to become the single most populous city in the
Iberian Peninsula on the eve of the sixteenth century. Zaragoza held
steady at 20k throughout the fifteenth century, anchoring the Aragonese
interior while commercial primacy belonged decisively to Valencia.

## Kingdom of Portugal and the Atlantic Isles

Encompassing 85.325,6 km² across mainland Portugal together with Madeira
and the Azores, the western sub-region expanded by 87,69% across the
chapter span, rising from 650k in 711AD (7,3 per km²) to 820k in 1000AD
(9,21 per km²), 1,08M in 1200AD (12,13 per km²) and 1,35M in 1347AD
(15,16 per km²), before contracting 9,63% to 1,22M in 1492AD (13,7 per
km²). Its 61 recorded localities (47 Buringh urban series and 14
Pleiades ancient-to-mediaeval sites) reflect the persistent north-south
contrast between densely settled Minho-Douro smallholdings and sparsely
populated Alentejo estates.

During the early mediaeval centuries, northern Portugal shared the wet
Atlantic-upland rye and chestnut ecology of neighbouring Galicia
[\[10\]](#ref-Seabra2023Introduction), while southern Gharb al-Andalus —
centred on Santarém, Lisboa, Evora, Beja and Silves — belonged to the
Islamic agrarian and urban sphere [\[3\]](#ref-Macroberts2024Shrouded).
Lisboa remained a steady provincial port of roughly 15k from 750AD to
1200AD, while Porto hovered between 7k and 10k. Afonso Henriques’s
capture of Santarém and Lisboa in 1147AD, aided by northern European
crusader fleets, and the final annexation of the Algarve in 1249AD
unified the Portuguese littoral and oriented the kingdom towards
Atlantic shipping, salt, wine, cod and coastal whaling and fishing
economies with deep pre-mediaeval roots
[\[18\]](#ref-Nabais2024Zooarchaeology). Once Afonso III transferred the
royal capital from Coimbra to Lisboa in 1255AD, urban growth accelerated
dramatically: Lisboa more than doubled from 15k in 1200AD to 35k in
1300AD, reached 43,3k in 1347AD, weathered the 1348AD plague and the
1383-1385AD succession crisis to hit 55k in 1400AD, and climbed to 64,1k
in 1492AD — second only to Valencia across all Iberia. Porto likewise
rebounded from 6k in 1300AD to 18k in 1400AD (14,3k in 1492AD). Despite
rural labour shortages codified in the 1375AD *Lei das Sesmarias* after
recurring plague mortality, Portugal possessed enough maritime surplus
by the fifteenth century to settle Madeira (from 1419AD, rapidly
becoming Europe’s premier sugar-exporting island) and the Azores (from
the 1430s), laying the demographic springboard for the Atlantic empire.

## Balearic and Canary Islands

Although the Balearic and Canary Islands represent only 7.482 km² (1,26%
of the chapter area), they recorded the highest population densities of
any Iberian sub-region after 1200AD: rising from 95k in 711AD (12,7 per
km²) to 135k in 1000AD (18,04 per km²), 165k in 1200AD (22,05 per km²)
and 195k in 1347AD (26,06 per km²), before easing 5,13% to 185k in
1492AD (24,73 per km²). Their trajectory merges two distinct island
histories. In the Mediterranean, Mallorca, Menorca and Ibiza prospered
under Umayyad, Taifa and Almoravid rule as maritime entrepôts and
irrigated agricultural islands before James I’s 1229-1231AD conquest
replaced much of the Muslim population with Catalan colonists; Mallorca
then suffered devastating Black Death losses in 1348AD and acute
fourteenth-century peasant unrest (*forans* against urban elites in
Palma). In the Atlantic, the seven Canary Islands remained home to
Berber-descended indigenous Guanche and Canario pastoral-agrarian
communities — perhaps 60k to 80k inhabitants prior to sustained European
contact — until Norman-Castilian expeditions beginning in 1402AD
(Lanzarote, Fuerteventura, El Hierro) and crown campaigns on Gran
Canaria (1478-1483AD), La Palma (1492-1493AD) and Tenerife (1494-1496AD)
triggered demographic collapse from introduced Old World pathogens,
enslavement and warfare, partially offset by arriving Castilian,
Portuguese and Genoese sugar colonists. As a marginal insular frontier
exposed to abrupt ecological and epidemiological shocks, the Canarian
transition parallels other mediaeval North Atlantic island
vulnerabilities [\[19\]](#ref-Dugmore2012).

## Urban Hierarchy and Rank-Size Structure

Figure 2 and Table 4 chart the structural transformation of Iberia’s
urban apex between 800AD and 1492AD. Across all 508 recorded
settlements, peak populations follow a disciplined rank-size gradient
($\alpha = 0,78$, $R^2 = 0,99$), reflecting a multi-polar urban system
distributed across five regional spheres rather than a single primate
capital. Yet the identity of the leading cities changed dramatically
over time. In 1000AD, the top five cities in Iberia were Cordoba (100k),
Sevilla (72k), Toledo (37k), Merida (30k) and Jaen (20k) — all five
ruled by the Umayyad Caliphate. By 1300AD, following the
thirteenth-century Reconquista, the leading five comprised Sevilla
(90k), Cordoba (60k), Granada (50k), Barcelona (48k) and Toledo (42k),
reflecting a temporary equilibrium between Castilian Andalusia, Nasrid
Granada, Catalan Mediterranean trade and the Meseta. By 1492AD, the
ranking had inverted once more: Valencia (71,3k) and Lisboa (64,1k) led
the entire peninsula, followed by Sevilla (46,6k), Cordoba (35,4k),
Toledo (32,9k), Valladolid (29k), Granada (23,7k), Plasencia (23k), Jaen
(21,7k) and Barcelona (21,1k).

This urban rotation reflects a decisive economic shift. Early mediaeval
Iberia’s wealth resided in inland hydraulic river valleys — the middle
Guadalquivir, the Guadiana and the Tagus — integrated into the
trans-Mediterranean Islamic market. Late mediaeval Iberia’s
fastest-growing cities lay instead along the eastern and western
maritime rims (Valencia and Lisboa) and across the commercial fair towns
of Old and New Castile (Valladolid, Medina del Campo, Plasencia, Alcazar
de San Juan). At the same time, the total number of Iberian towns
exceeding 5k inhabitants expanded nearly threefold from 31 in 1000AD to

Iberian history.

# Limitations

Five evidentiary and cartographic limitations bound the precision of
these reconstructions. First, the total absence of surviving household
censuses in Al-Andalus and pre-1350AD Christian Iberia means that 711AD,
1000AD and 1200AD sub-regional totals carry wider uncertainty envelopes
(roughly plus or minus 15% to 20%) than the 1347AD and 1492AD benchmarks
(roughly plus or minus 10%), where Aragonese *fogatges* and
Castilian-Portuguese hearth surveys anchor rural densities
[\[1\]](#ref-Russell1958)[\[7\]](#ref-Buringh2021). Second,
household-size multipliers used to convert fiscal hearths (*fuegos* and
*vecinos*) into individuals varied across time and social strata —
falling towards 3,8 to 4 immediately after severe plague waves and
rising above 4,5 in expanding peasant frontiers — while noble,
ecclesiastical, enslaved and itinerant groups were inconsistently
enumerated. Third, the 1492AD benchmark captures Iberia at the exact
threshold of the Alhambra Decree expelling the Sephardic Jewish
population and immediately before the forced conversions and
sixteenth-century uprisings of the Moriscos; depending on whether
departing Jewish households (most modern scholarship places actual
emigrants between 40k and 100k, with a portion converting or returning
via Portugal) are counted on 1 January or 31 December 1492AD, late-year
urban figures in Toledo, Sevilla, Zaragoza and Barcelona shift
noticeably [\[14\]](#ref-Martinezarino2020Jewish).

Fourth, static raster-polygon boundaries introduce minor territorial
edge artefacts: Palma de Mallorca falls inside the Aragon-Catalonia
coastal mask, and nine northern Pyrenean and Roussillon localities
located inside modern France (reflecting historical Aragonese-Catalan
sovereignty over Roussillon and Cerdagne until 1659AD, alongside narrow
western Pyrenean mask overlaps such as Hasparren and Coutances) plus
Andorra la Vella enter the northern sub-regions. Fifth, whereas the 307
Buringh urban series capture dynamic century-by-century growth and
contraction, the 192 Pleiades continuous settlements are modelled at
conservative floor tiers (predominantly 510 inhabitants) and the 9
GeoNames Canary Islands anchors carry fixed municipal tiers (including
Telde at 14,8k) across 750-1492AD; accordingly, macro-regional totals in
the Canary Islands rely on ethnohistorical and colonial estimates rather
than summed static gazetteer entries.

# Conclusion

Over the 781 years between 711AD and 1492AD, Iberia added nearly 4M
inhabitants, lifting its total from 5M to 8,98M after cresting at 9,2M
on the eve of the Black Death. Far from representing uniform gradualism,
those centuries inverted the peninsula’s demographic geography. The
Umayyad Caliphate lifted southern Al-Andalus to densities of 25,3
inhabitants per km² and built Europe’s foremost western metropolis at
Cordoba (100k), yet political fragmentation, the thirteenth-century
*repartimientos* and plague erosion pared the south back to 23,93% of
peninsular headcount by 1492AD. Across the same span, northern Christian
*repoblación*, upland cereal adaptation, transhumant wool commerce and
fifteenth-century rural resilience pushed the Crown of Castile-Leon up
by 154,84% to 3,95M — 43,96% of all Iberia and the sole sub-region to
surpass its 1347AD ceiling before 1500AD. Meanwhile, maritime Valencia
(71,3k) and Lisboa (64,1k) displaced Cordoba and Barcelona at the apex
of a polycentric urban network ($\alpha = 0,78$) spanning 80 towns of 5k
or more inhabitants. On the threshold of overseas empire, Iberia’s
demographic centre of gravity stood firmly anchored on the Castilian
Meseta and its Atlantic and Levantine ports, ready to supply the
settlers, mariners and fiscal base of the early modern Iberian crowns.
