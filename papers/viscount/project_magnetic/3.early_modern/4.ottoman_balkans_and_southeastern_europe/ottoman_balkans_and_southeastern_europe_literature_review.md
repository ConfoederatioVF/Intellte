# Abstract

Between the Ottoman crossing of the Dardanelles in 1354 and the
diplomatic settlement of the Greek and Danubian crises in 1830, the
population of South-Eastern Europe rose two-and-a-half-fold from an
impoverished post-plague floor to nearly 20M inhabitants. Across 7
demographic sub-regions encompassing 809.710,7 km² — Bosnia,
Herzegovina, Croatia and Slovenia; Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo;
Albania and Macedonia; Greece and the Aegean Islands; Bulgaria;
Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania; and Eastern Thrace — aggregate
headcount expanded from 7,82M in 1400 (9,66 inhabitants per km²) to
9,31M in 1500, 10,81M in 1600, 11,48M in 1700, 17,12M in 1800 and 19,91M
in 1830 (24,59 inhabitants per km²)
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997). Growth unfolded in
three sharply distinct phases: steady recovery and urban reconstruction
under the classical Ottoman *tahrir* fiscal regime during the fifteenth
and sixteenth centuries (+38,2% between 1400 and 1600); prolonged
stagnation during the seventeenth century (+6,2% overall), marked by
absolute wartime contractions in Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo (-4,1%)
and Greece (-4,2%) during the Cretan, Morean and Great Turkish wars; and
an accelerating agrarian surge after 1700 (+73,4% by 1830), propelled by
maize diffusion across the Lower Danube plains, Habsburg frontier
colonisation and declining epidemic intensity
[\[3\]](#ref-Alfani2013)[\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development)[\[5\]](#ref-Ploscaru2022Union).
Eastern Thrace quadrupled in density to 55,72 inhabitants per km² around
imperial Istanbul (700k in 1700) and Edirne (150k in 1600), while
Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania grew 3,51-fold from 1,95M to 6,85M,
lifting their share of the macro-region from 24,9% to 34,4%. This
chapter harmonises Ottoman household registers, *cizye* poll-tax rolls,
Venetian and Danubian fiscal surveys, a 635-settlement gazetteer led by
148 longitudinal urban series [\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021) and an
independent 1820 Habsburg Crownlands historical GIS benchmark covering
15 administrative provinces and 26,32M inhabitants
[\[7\]](#ref-HistoGIS2022).

# Background

## Ottoman Conquest, Rumelian Consolidation and the Tahrir Fiscal Order (1350-1600)

In 1350 South-Eastern Europe was a politically fractured, plague-thinned
peninsula. The Byzantine Empire had shrunk to Constantinople, Thrace,
Salonica and the Despotate of the Morea; Stephen Dušan’s Serbian kingdom
was on the verge of breaking into rival principalities after his death
in 1355; the Second Bulgarian Empire had split into Tarnovo and Vidin;
and Venetian, Genoese and Frankish lordships competed along the Adriatic
and Aegean coasts. The Black Death of 1347-1352 struck maritime ports
such as Constantinople, Salonica, Dubrovnik and Candia first before
penetrating inland river corridors, reducing regional population well
below its thirteenth-century peak.

Into this fractured landscape crossed the Ottomans. Seizing Gallipoli in
1354 after an earthquake collapsed its walls, Ottoman forces moved
rapidly up the Maritsa valley, took Adrianople (Edirne) in 1362 and made
it their European seat of government. Victory on the Maritsa in 1371
broke the Serbian lords of Macedonia; the Battle of Kosovo Polje in 1389
reduced northern Serbia to vassalage; Tarnovo fell in 1393; and the
crusade of Nicopolis was crushed on the Danube in 1396. After a brief
interregnum following Timur’s invasion of Anatolia in 1402, Ottoman
armies absorbed Salonica (1430), stormed Constantinople in May 1453,
extinguished the Serbian Despotate at Smederevo (1459), annexed the
Morea (1460) and Bosnia (1463), subdued Herzegovina (1482) and brought
Wallachia and Moldavia under tributary suzerainty. Under Süleyman the
Magnificent, the capture of Belgrade in 1521 and the victory at Mohács
in 1526 pushed the Ottoman military frontier onto the Middle Danube,
leaving only Habsburg Carniola, Styria, Civil Croatia, Venetian Dalmatia
and the mountain fastnesses of Montenegro outside direct imperial
control
[\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development)[\[8\]](#ref-Sener2019Structural).

Demographically, Ottoman unification ended two centuries of endemic
petty warfare across the Balkan interior and introduced a remarkably
systematic fiscal administration. Within decades of annexing each
province, imperial scribes compiled detailed cadastral registers
(*tahrir defters*) listing villages, arable plots, agricultural tithes
and taxable households (*hane*), alongside separate rolls for the
non-Muslim poll tax (*cizye*) and urban craft wards (*mahalles*)
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[9\]](#ref-Mutaf2026Quantifying)[\[10\]](#ref-Ozates2024Dependency).
Where war or plague had emptied strategic plains — particularly Eastern
Thrace, the Maritsa valley, Dobruja and eastern Macedonia — the state
applied *sürgün*, the planned transfer of Turkish peasant farmers, Yörük
pastoralists and Anatolian artisans into Rumelia. Mehmed II applied the
same instrument on an imperial scale after 1453 to repopulate ruined
Constantinople, drawing Greek, Armenian, Turkish and Jewish households
from Thrace, Anatolia, Trebizond and the Aegean islands, later augmented
by tens of thousands of Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain in 1492 who
settled mainly in Istanbul, Salonica and Edirne
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[11\]](#ref-Gokce2018Iznik).

This imperial peace did not erase the demographic substrate of the
peninsula. Autosomal and haploid genetic surveys confirm strong
underlying continuity across Western Balkan populations despite
centuries of imperial migration and frontier movement
[\[12\]](#ref-Kovacevic2014Standing), while genomic and archival studies
alike trace how the Balkans functioned as the primary European gateway
for Roma communities who were explicitly enumerated in Ottoman *tahrir*
and *cizye* rolls as settled craftsmen, metalworkers and military
auxiliaries
[\[13\]](#ref-Fontporterias2019European)[\[14\]](#ref-Marushiakova2020Gypsy).
Between 1400 and 1600, the combination of internal security, restored
overland trade along the Via Militaris and Via Egnatia, urban *vakıf*
(charitable endowment) construction and repopulation of empty alluvial
plains lifted regional population from 7,82M to 10,81M — an increase of
2,99M inhabitants (+38,2%) over two centuries
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[15\]](#ref-Kasumovic2026Ottoman).

## Seventeenth-Century Crisis, Habsburg-Ottoman Wars and the Great Serbian Migrations (1600-1718)

After 1600, the sixteenth-century expansion stalled across almost the
entire peninsula. The causes were partly climatic and epidemiological,
partly institutional and military. Cooler, wetter summers associated
with the Little Ice Age shortened grain harvests across the Dinaric,
Rhodope and Carpathian uplands, while recurring plague waves struck
Adriatic, Aegean and Danubian port towns with severe mortality
[\[3\]](#ref-Alfani2013). Simultaneously, the classical *timar*
prebendal system broke down as inflation eroded fixed cavalry stipends,
cash tax farming (*iltizam*) spread and emergency levies (*avarız*)
became permanent annual exactions. Provincial garrisons and unpaid
irregulars (*levend*) preyed on lowland villages, prompting peasants to
abandon exposed highway valleys for higher karst and mountain hamlets
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997).

Prolonged warfare turned demographic stagnation into regional
contraction. The Long Turkish War (1593-1606) ravaged the
Hungarian-Croatian borderlands and Wallachia. Between 1645 and 1669, the
Cretan War pitted Ottoman and Venetian fleets across the Aegean for a
quarter of a century, disrupting insular trade and depopulating Crete
and several Cycladic islands. Worse followed after the failure of the
Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683. During the Great Turkish War
(1683-1699), Habsburg armies drove down the Danube through Buda (1686)
and Belgrade (1688) as far south as Niš, Priština and Skopje, while
Venetian forces under Francesco Morosini invaded the Peloponnese,
shelling the Parthenon in Athens in 1687 and occupying the Morea until
1715.

When an Ottoman counter-offensive rolled back the Habsburg advance in
1690, Patriarch Arsenije III Čarnojević led the Great Serbian Migration
(*Velika Seoba Srba*) north across the Sava and Danube into Syrmia,
Slavonia, Bačka and southern Hungary, where Emperor Leopold I granted
the refugees ecclesiastical autonomy and frontier land in exchange for
military service along the *Vojna Krajina*. Tens of thousands of
households left Kosovo, Metohija, the Morava valley and northern
Macedonia; a second wartime exodus followed in 1737-1739 under Patriarch
Arsenije IV after the Austrians lost Belgrade again
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development). In the
depopulated plains of Kosovo and western Macedonia, highland Albanian
pastoralists gradually descended from the northern Albanian Alps to take
up empty arable holdings. Across the study domain as a whole, population
barely moved from 10,81M in 1600 to 11,48M in 1700 (+6,2%), and two of
the seven sub-regions suffered outright net declines: Serbia, Montenegro
and Kosovo fell from 1,48M to 1,42M (-4,1%), while Greece and the Aegean
Islands dropped from 1,65M to 1,58M (-4,2%).

## Eighteenth-Century Agrarian Expansion, Danubian Frontiers and Early National Uprisings (1718-1830)

The Treaties of Karlowitz (1699) and Passarowitz (1718) redrew the
geopolitical map of South-Eastern Europe. The Habsburgs consolidated
Transylvania, Slavonia, the Banat of Temesvár and (temporarily, until
1739) northern Serbia, establishing a permanent quarantine cordon
(*Sanitätskordon*) along the Military Frontier that progressively
curtailed plague transmission into Central Europe
[\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development). Vienna also declared Trieste and
Fiume imperial free ports in 1719, transforming the northern Adriatic
coast into a commercial outlet for Carniola, Styria and Hungary.

Inside both the Habsburg perimeter and the Ottoman Danubian and Rumelian
provinces, the eighteenth century brought an extraordinary demographic
rebound. Aggregate population leaped from 11,48M in 1700 to 17,12M in
1800 (+49,1%, adding 5,64M people in a single century) and climbed a
further 2,79M to 19,91M by 1830. The decisive ecological engine of this
surge was the rapid diffusion of New World maize across Wallachia,
Moldavia, Transylvania, Serbia, Bosnia and Bulgaria
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997). Maize yielded far more calories per hectare
than traditional wheat or millet, thrived on damp river-bottom soils and
mountain clearings, required lower seeding ratios and — crucially in
Ottoman and Phanariot provinces — escaped imperial grain purchase
monopolies (*mübaya’a*) aimed at provisioning Istanbul with wheat and
barley. Combined with extensive pig-rearing in the oak forests of
Šumadija and Slavonia, maize sharply lowered the frequency of
subsistence famines.

Yet eighteenth-century growth unfolded amid deep institutional
volatility. In the Ottoman interior, central authority weakened in
favour of provincial magnates (*ayans*) such as Osman Pazvantoğlu at
Vidin and Ali Pasha of Tepelena at Ioannina, while mercenary bands
(*kırcalı*) devastated Thracian and Bulgarian lowlands during the 1790s,
accelerating a demographic shift from open plains into fortified upland
textile and craft towns along the Stara Planina and Rhodope ranges
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[15\]](#ref-Kasumovic2026Ottoman).
In Wallachia and Moldavia, Greek Phanariot princes appointed by the
Porte governed tributary economies repeatedly crossed by Russian and
Austrian armies in 1768-1774, 1787-1792, 1806-1812 (when Russia annexed
eastern Moldavia as Bessarabia) and 1828-1829
[\[5\]](#ref-Ploscaru2022Union). Meanwhile, Serbian peasants rose
against renegade Janissaries in 1804 and secured an autonomous
Principality of Serbia by 1815-1830, while the Greek War of Independence
(1821-1829) inflicted severe demographic losses across the Peloponnese,
Central Greece and the eastern Aegean before Great Power intervention
and the Treaty of Adrianople (1829) established an independent Greek
kingdom and ended Ottoman commercial monopolies on the Lower Danube. By
1830, South-Eastern Europe stood at 19,91M inhabitants, poised between
its pre-industrial agrarian equilibrium and the state-building, railway
and census era of the later nineteenth century
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[16\]](#ref-Emini2022Efforts).

# Methodology

## Territorial Domain, Sub-Regional Partition and Areal Measurement

The chapter domain spans 809.710,7 km² across the Balkan Peninsula, the
Carpathian-Danubian crescent and Eastern Thrace, evaluated at six
historical benchmarks: 1400, 1500, 1600, 1700, 1800 and 1830. Because
imperial borders between the Ottoman Empire, the Habsburg Monarchy, the
Republic of Venice and the Russian Empire shifted repeatedly after 1683
[\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development), fixing demographic time series to
time-invariant geographic polygons is essential to prevent boundary
changes from masquerading as population growth or collapse.

Accordingly, MAGNETIC partitions the domain into 7 contiguous equal-area
sub-regions aligned with physical geography and modern statistical
boundaries: (1) Bosnia, Herzegovina, Croatia and Slovenia (126.176,8
km²); (2) Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo (101.933,8 km²); (3) Albania and
Macedonia (53.548,9 km²); (4) Greece and the Aegean Islands (130.332,2
km²); (5) Bulgaria (112.883,7 km²); (6) Wallachia, Moldavia and
Transylvania (261.865,2 km²); and (7) Eastern Thrace or Turkey-in-Europe
(22.970,2 km²). Notice two boundary conventions that keep regional
comparisons consistent: Eastern Thrace encompasses European Istanbul
(Stamboul, Galata and Pera) and the historic Thracian hinterland up to
the Maritsa, while Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania unites the
Romanian-speaking Danubian Principalities with the Carpathian basin of
Transylvania and eastern Moldavia (Bessarabia) so that cross-Carpathian
migration and Habsburg-Ottoman frontier exchanges remain internal to a
single macro-unit.

## Ottoman Household Multipliers, Cizye Calibration and Settlement Ingestion

Pre-1830 population estimation in South-Eastern Europe draws on four
complementary archival traditions, each requiring explicit demographic
calibration. First, for the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, Ottoman
*tahrir defters* enumerate adult male taxpayers by village and urban
quarter (*mahalle*), distinguishing married householders (*hane*),
unmarried adult males (*mücerred*) and widows (*bive*)
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983). Converting household counts into total
population requires a demographic multiplier. Classic Ottoman
historiography pioneered by Ömer Lütfi Barkan and refined by Nikolai
Todorov [\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983) applies a base coefficient of 4,5 to 5
persons per rural *hane*, adjusted upward toward 5,2 to 5,5 in Dinaric,
Serbian and Bulgarian zones characterised by extended patrilocal
*zadruga* households and downward to 3,8 to 4,2 in crowded commercial
quarters of Istanbul, Salonica and Edirne
[\[17\]](#ref-Reher1990)[\[18\]](#ref-DeVries1984). Unenumerated strata
— *askeri* military-administrative elites, garrison Janissaries,
religious officials, *vakıf* dependants, exempt *derbendci* pass-guards
and mobile Vlach or Yörük pastoralists — typically add 10% to 15% above
raw fiscal totals.

Second, for the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, as regular
*tahrir* cadastral sweeps gave way to lump-sum *avarız-hane* tax units
(where one fiscal unit could bundle three to fifteen real households
depending on regional wealth) and individual *cizye* certificates
(reformed in 1691 to tax every adult non-Muslim male directly),
aggregate reconstruction triangulates fiscal receipts against Ottoman
*kadi* court registers (*şer’iyye sicilleri*), Venetian cadastral
censuses — most notably the Grimani census of the Peloponnese in 1700 —
and local ecclesiastical parish rolls
[\[3\]](#ref-Alfani2013)[\[9\]](#ref-Mutaf2026Quantifying)[\[10\]](#ref-Ozates2024Dependency).
Third, from the late eighteenth century through 1830, systematic state
enumerations emerge along the northern and western perimeter: the
Habsburg Josephinian conscriptions of 1784-1787, the 1820 Austrian
Empire Crownland enumerations [\[7\]](#ref-HistoGIS2022), the 1828-1831
Ottoman *nüfus* male-population survey under Mahmud II, early Wallachian
and Moldavian *catagrafii* fiscal censuses (1831-1832) and the first
post-independence Serbian (1834) and Greek (1828-1838) headcounts
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[5\]](#ref-Ploscaru2022Union).

Fourth, regional demographic aggregates are paired with a spatially
explicit settlement gazetteer of 6.637 catalogued towns and
municipalities spanning 16 modern national territories (Table ). Primary
urban trajectories derive from Eltjo Buringh’s European city database
(148 longitudinal urban series from 1350 to 1830)
[\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021), complemented by 487 longitudinal municipal
anchors drawn from eight national statistical census registries
(Slovenia 129, Romania 122, Greece 111, Bulgaria 55, Serbia 43, Croatia
22, Hungary 4 and Austria 1), with zero crowd-sourced fallback records
[\[19\]](#ref-Dakic2025Citizen).

# Validation

## Historical GIS Crownland Enumerations (1820 Habsburg Cadastral Benchmark)

A decisive strength of late-epoch demographic calibration in
South-Eastern Europe is the availability of sub-administrative
historical GIS enumerations compiled by the Austrian Academy of Sciences
for the Austrian Empire in 1820 [\[7\]](#ref-HistoGIS2022). Because the
Habsburg Monarchy governed Slovenia, Istria, Dalmatia, Civil and
Military Croatia, Slavonia, Syrmia, Bačka, the Banat and Transylvania
directly at the close of our study window, these 1820 crownland polygons
provide an independent cadastral check against Ottoman-era
extrapolations across roughly one-third of the chapter’s northern and
Adriatic landmass.

In aggregate, the 1820 HistoGIS Austrian Empire regime encompasses 44
imperial crownlands totalling 10,6M (10.599.998) inhabitants across
317.410 km², yielding an empire-wide mean density of 33,4 inhabitants
per km². Table presents all 15 crownland units alongside their spatial
relationship to the Balkan study domain.

Direct comparison of the five core perimeter crownlands in Table against
our sub-regional trajectories confirms tight demographic coherence. In
1820, the Grand Principality of Transylvania (*Großfürstentum
Siebenbürgen*) recorded 1,75M inhabitants across 68.848 km² — a density
of 25,42 inhabitants per km². In our series for Wallachia, Moldavia and
Transylvania, regional density moves from 22,34 inhabitants per km² in
1800 to 26,16 in 1830, interpolating to 24,89 inhabitants per km² in
1820 — within 2,1% of the Habsburg cadastral count. Similarly, the five
Balkan-Adriatic crownlands combined (Transylvania, Illyria, Croatia,
Slavonia and Dalmatia) held 4,05M inhabitants on 134.656 km² (30,07
inhabitants per km²). Individually, rugged Dalmatia (21,31 inhabitants
per km²) and marshy Slavonia (25,89 inhabitants per km²) closely bracket
our Bosnia, Herzegovina, Croatia and Slovenia sub-region (21,79 in 1800
and 24,96 in 1830), whereas densely settled Civil Croatia (47,92) and
Illyria (39,6, incorporating Carniola and the booming port of Trieste)
balance the far thinner karst uplands of Ottoman Bosnia and Herzegovina
(roughly 14 to 16 inhabitants per km² in 1820)
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development).

Equally instructive is the density gradient separating the Habsburg
Balkan perimeter from the monarchy’s western industrial and alluvial
cores. In 1820, Lombardy-Venetia stood at 99,46 inhabitants per km²,
Bohemia at 65,73 and Moravia at 64,26 — roughly triple the South-Eastern
European mean of 24,59 inhabitants per km² in 1830. Even the Kingdom of
Hungary at 42,05 inhabitants per km² (incorporating densely recolonised
southern counties in Bačka and the Banat) surpassed the Ottoman Danubian
and Rumelian average by more than 70%. That sharp east-west gradient
aligns directly with what independent agrarian and fiscal historians
observe across the Karlowitz-Sistova frontier
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[20\]](#ref-PfisterFertig2010).

## Cross-Checks Against Comparative European Demographic Baselines

Placed alongside Europe’s major early-modern demographic
reconstructions, the Ottoman Balkans occupy a coherent intermediate
position between densely farmed Atlantic-Mediterranean cores and
sparsely populated Boreal frontiers. In 1600, when France averaged
roughly 36 inhabitants per km² [\[21\]](#ref-Dupaquier1988), England
approached 32 inhabitants per km² [\[22\]](#ref-WrigleySchofield1981)
and northern Italy exceeded 50 inhabitants per km² despite recurrent
plague shocks [\[3\]](#ref-Alfani2013), South-Eastern Europe averaged
13,35 inhabitants per km² — comparable to the arid meseta of Castile
[\[17\]](#ref-Reher1990) and roughly half the density of the German
lands prior to the Thirty Years’ War [\[20\]](#ref-PfisterFertig2010).

Two structural factors explain both this lower seventeenth-century
ceiling and the region’s unusually rapid eighteenth-century
acceleration. First, mountainous topography across the Dinaric Alps,
Pindus, Rhodope, Stara Planina and Carpathian arcs restricted contiguous
ploughland, favouring transhumant sheep and goat pastoralism over
intensive three-field grain husbandry
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[23\]](#ref-Cardenas2026Climatic). Second,
recurrent frontier warfare along the Sava-Danube and Aegean corridors
periodically emptied fertile alluvial basins — notably the Morava
valley, Slavonia, southern Serbia and the Wallachian plain — keeping
population well below ecological carrying capacity
[\[24\]](#ref-Kubasch2025Empirical). Once Habsburg-Ottoman borders
stabilised after 1718 and maize spread across peasant holdings,
South-Eastern Europe grew by 49,1% between 1700 and 1800, matching or
exceeding the eighteenth-century expansion of Sweden, Denmark and
England [\[22\]](#ref-WrigleySchofield1981)[\[25\]](#ref-Gille1949) even
while remaining an overwhelmingly rural society.

Urban ratios provide a final consistency check. In Jan de Vries’s
classic benchmark for Western and Central Europe
[\[18\]](#ref-DeVries1984), cities above 10k inhabitants accounted for
roughly 7% to 10% of total population in 1600-1700 within a balanced
polycentric rank-size hierarchy. In the Ottoman Balkans, by contrast,
Istanbul alone held 680k to 700k people between 1600 and 1700 — 6,1% of
the entire 11,48M macro-regional population — while Edirne added another
100k to 150k. Excluding Eastern Thrace, the urban share across the
remaining six Balkan sub-regions remained modest (roughly 5% to 7% in
1700), concentrated in provincial capitals, caravan entrepôts and
maritime ports such as Salonica, Belgrade, Sofia, Sarajevo, Bukarest,
Plovdiv and Shkodër [\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021).

# Results

## Aggregate Demographic Trajectory Across South-Eastern Europe (1400-1830)

Between 1400 and 1830, the aggregate population of the Ottoman Balkans
and South-Eastern Europe increased by 12,09M people, rising 2,55-fold
from 7,82M to 19,91M at a long-run compound growth rate of 0,22% per
annum. Figure displays the combined macro-regional curve alongside the
seven underlying sub-regional paths across all six benchmarks, while
Table reports the exact harmonised headcounts.

<img src="figures/ottoman_balkans_and_southeastern_europe_population_trajectory.png" width="3266" />

Viewed century by century, the trajectory falls into four distinct
demographic regimes. From 1400 to 1500, recovery from the Black Death
and early Ottoman pacification added 1,49M inhabitants (+19,1%, or 0,17%
per annum), lifting regional density from 9,66 to 11,5 inhabitants per
km². Between 1500 and 1600, imperial consolidation under Selim I and
Süleyman the Magnificent sustained a similar absolute gain of 1,5M
inhabitants (+16,1%, or 0,15% per annum), carrying the total past 10,81M
(13,35 inhabitants per km²).

The seventeenth century broke that upward momentum abruptly. Between
1600 and 1700, net population across 809.710,7 km² crept upward by just
670k persons (+6,2%, or 0,06% per annum) to 11,48M (14,18 inhabitants
per km²). Beneath that near-flat continental curve lay stark regional
divergence: Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania (+400k) and Bosnia,
Herzegovina, Croatia and Slovenia (+200k) kept growing slowly, whereas
Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo (-60k) and Greece and the Aegean Islands
(-70k) contracted under the combined blows of war, refugee flight and
plague.

After 1700, the demographic curve inflected sharply upward. Between 1700
and 1800, total population jumped by 5,64M (+49,1%, or 0,4% per annum)
to reach 17,12M (21,14 inhabitants per km²). Every single sub-region
participated in this eighteenth-century expansion, though at sharply
unequal speeds: Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania surged by 80%
(+2,6M), Bulgaria grew by 48,3% (+700k), Bosnia, Herzegovina, Croatia
and Slovenia rose by 41% (+800k), Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo
rebounded by 37,3% (+530k), Greece recovered by 36,1% (+570k), Albania
and Macedonia gained 27,3% (+240k) and Eastern Thrace increased by 21,1%
(+200k). During the final thirty years from 1800 to 1830, growth
accelerated further in annual terms to 0,5% per annum, adding 2,79M
inhabitants (+16,3%) to reach 19,91M despite the Napoleonic Adriatic
wars, the Serbian uprisings, the Russo-Turkish war of 1828-1829 and the
devastating Greek War of Independence.

## The Western Balkans: Bosnia, Herzegovina, Croatia, Slovenia, Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo

The northwestern sector — Bosnia, Herzegovina, Croatia and Slovenia
(126.176,8 km², 170 catalogued settlements) — expanded steadily from
1,35M in 1400 (10,7 inhabitants per km²) to 1,55M in 1500, 1,75M in
1600, 1,95M in 1700, 2,75M in 1800 and 3,15M in 1830 (24,96 inhabitants
per km²), a 2,33-fold increase over the epoch. Its internal geography
was split sharply along the Triplex Confinium where Ottoman, Habsburg
and Venetian jurisdictions met [\[4\]](#ref-Balta2021Development). In
Carniola, Lower Styria and Civil Croatia, Alpine and Pannonian
agriculture sustained dense peasant villages anchored by Ljubljana,
Maribor and Zagreb, while Venetian Dalmatia relied on coastal
viticulture, shipping and maritime trade centred on Split, Zadar and the
independent merchant republic of Dubrovnik.

Inside Ottoman Bosnia and Herzegovina, steep karst valleys limited
arable tillage [\[23\]](#ref-Cardenas2026Climatic), yet widespread
conversion of local landowners and urban craftsmen to Islam fostered
vigorous Ottoman town-building through *vakıf* endowments
[\[15\]](#ref-Kasumovic2026Ottoman). Sarajevo — barely a hamlet of 3k
souls in 1400 — grew into a major regional centre of 10k in 1600, 20k in
1700 (despite Prince Eugene of Savoy’s destructive raid of 1697), 40k in
1800 and 45,7k in 1830 [\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021). Even more dramatic was
the rise of Habsburg Trieste on the Adriatic rim: stagnating between 3k
and 6k from 1400 to 1700 under Venetian commercial encirclement, Trieste
surged after receiving free-port status in 1719 to 12k in 1750, 25k in
1800 and 43,9k in 1830, drawing migrants from Carniola, Istria, Dalmatia
and northern Italy.

Immediately to the east, Serbia, Montenegro and Kosovo (101.933,8 km²,
63 catalogued settlements) traced the most turbulent frontier curve in
South-Eastern Europe, growing 1,88-fold overall — the lowest cumulative
multiple of all seven sub-regions — from 1,25M in 1400 (12,26
inhabitants per km²) to 1,38M in 1500 and 1,48M in 1600 before falling
to 1,42M in 1700 (13,93 inhabitants per km²), then rebounding strongly
to 1,95M in 1800 and 2,35M in 1830 (23,05 inhabitants per km²).
Sixteenth-century Ottoman stability had nourished silver-mining and
caravan centres such as Novo Brdo, Prizren, Niš and above all Belgrade,
which swelled from 30k in 1500 to 55k in 1600 as the empire’s primary
Danubian arsenal and grain entrepôt.

Thereafter, Serbia became Europe’s premier military shatterzone.
Repeated sieges of Belgrade in 1688, 1690, 1717, 1739 and 1789 — paired
with the Great Serbian Migrations of 1690 and 1737-1739 — drove Belgrade
down from 55k in 1600 to 17k in 1700, 9k in 1750 and barely 5k in 1800
[\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021). Conversely, across the Sava and Danube inside
Habsburg Hungary and Vojvodina, refugee inflows and state-sponsored
settlement turned once-empty frontier towns into booming agricultural
hubs: Szeged climbed from 4k in 1700 to 22k in 1800 and 35,6k in 1830,
while Subotica jumped from 2k in 1700 to 21k in 1800 and 34,5k in 1830.
South of the Sava, the forested Šumadija hills repopulated rapidly
during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries via Dinaric
upland immigration from Herzegovina and Montenegro, underpinning the
autonomous Serbian principality whose population recovery lifted
Belgrade back to 10,8k by 1830
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[12\]](#ref-Kovacevic2014Standing).

## The Southern Balkans: Albania, Macedonia, Greece and the Aegean Islands

Albania and Macedonia (53.548,9 km², 17 catalogued settlements) grew
steadily from 650k in 1400 (12,14 inhabitants per km²) to 720k in 1500,
820k in 1600, 880k in 1700, 1,12M in 1800 and 1,28M in 1830 (23,9
inhabitants per km²), nearly doubling (+96,9%) across four centuries.
Mountainous terrain around the Accursed Mountains, Lake Ohrid and the
Vardar watershed restricted extensive grain plains, yet pastoralism,
olive groves along the Ionian-Adriatic littoral and overland commerce
along the Via Egnatia sustained higher baseline densities than either
Bulgaria or Greece throughout the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[26\]](#ref-Trajanovski2010Native). Following
the suppression of Skanderbeg’s resistance in the late fifteenth
century, significant Albanian Christian populations emigrated across the
Adriatic to southern Italy (the *Arbëreshë*) and southward into Attica,
Boeotia and the Peloponnese (*Arvanites*). Within Albania and western
Macedonia itself, gradual conversion to Islam during the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries eased fiscal pressure under the *cizye* and opened
military-administrative careers across the empire, while seasonal male
labour migration (*gurbet*) remitted silver to highland villages.
Shkodër expanded from 2k in 1400 and 5k in 1600 to 23k in 1700 and 40k
in 1800 under the autonomous Bushati pashas, complementing inland
Ottoman administrative hubs at Bitola (Monastir), Skopje, Berat and
Elbasan [\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021).

Greece and the Aegean Islands (130.332,2 km², 134 catalogued
settlements) experienced a markedly more volatile maritime trajectory.
From 1,25M in 1400 (9,59 inhabitants per km²), population climbed to
1,45M in 1500 and 1,65M in 1600 (12,66 inhabitants per km²) as Ottoman
unification curtailed Latin-Byzantine feudal strife and Thessalian,
Macedonian and Peloponnesian valleys recovered. During the seventeenth
century, however, the twenty-four-year Cretan War (1645-1669), Venetian
raids, corsair depopulation of exposed Aegean islets, plague outbreaks
[\[3\]](#ref-Alfani2013) and the Morean War (1684-1699) reduced total
headcount to 1,58M in 1700 (12,12 inhabitants per km²).

Recovery during the eighteenth century lifted Greece to 2,15M by 1800
(16,5 inhabitants per km²), buoyed by a booming Greek-flagged merchant
marine operating under Russian and Ottoman protection after 1774,
commercial currant and silk exports from the Peloponnese and Chios
alongside upland textile prosperity in Thessaly and Epirus
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[15\]](#ref-Kasumovic2026Ottoman). Saloniki
(Thessaloniki) epitomised this maritime cycle: shrinking from 42k in
1400 to 19k in 1500 after the Ottoman conquest of 1430, it rebounded to
50k in 1600 after absorbing a massive Sephardic Jewish influx, dipped to
40k in 1700 and reached 70k across 1800-1830 as the premier commercial
port of the northern Aegean. By contrast, Athens declined from 35k in
1400 and 33k in 1600 to barely 10k-13k across 1700-1800 following
Morosini’s 1687 bombardment and prolonged insecurity, before rising to
20,8k by 1830 as the designated capital of the new Greek state, while
Serrai reached 32,9k [\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021). Between 1800 and 1830,
net Greek growth slowed to +14% (reaching 2,45M, or 18,8 inhabitants per
km²) owing to heavy wartime mortality, massacres on Chios (1822) and
Psara (1824) and Peloponnesian devastation during the Greek War of
Independence.

## Rumelian Core and Danubian Plain: Bulgaria, Wallachia, Moldavia, Transylvania and Eastern Thrace

Bulgaria (112.883,7 km², 76 catalogued settlements) grew 2,43-fold from
1,05M in 1400 (9,3 inhabitants per km²) to 1,18M in 1500, 1,38M in 1600,
1,45M in 1700, 2,15M in 1800 and 2,55M in 1830 (22,59 inhabitants per
km²). Lying directly astride the imperial military highway linking
Constantinople and Edirne to Belgrade, the Danubian plain and Thracian
valley absorbed substantial Anatolian Turkish and Tatar colonisation
during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries while Bulgarian peasants
maintained dense agricultural and sheep-rearing settlements in the Stara
Planina foothills and western basins
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[14\]](#ref-Marushiakova2020Gypsy). Despite
severe disruption from *kırcalı* brigandage in the 1790s and the wartime
flight of tens of thousands of Bulgarians into Wallachia and Russian
Bessarabia during the Russo-Turkish war of 1828-1829, strong natural
increase and upland woollen-cloth proto-industry (supplying uniforms for
the Ottoman army) lifted urban centres across the province
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997). Sofia rose from 4k in 1400 and 6k in 1600 to
17k in 1700 and 46k in 1800 (44,2k in 1830); Plovdiv (Filibe) grew from
8k in 1400 to 15k in 1600, 22k in 1700 and 30k across 1750-1830; and the
Danubian river ports of Vidin (25k in 1800, 23,8k in 1830) and Ruse (19k
in 1800, 22,9k in 1830) became major Ottoman border strongholds and
grain depots.

North of the Danube, Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania (261.865,2
km², 171 catalogued settlements) staged the single largest demographic
transformation in South-Eastern Europe. In 1400, this vast
Carpathian-Danubian expanse was the most thinly populated sub-region in
the entire chapter, holding 1,95M inhabitants at just 7,45 inhabitants
per km² — exposed to Crimean Tatar slave raids across the Budjak steppe
and recurrent Hungarian-Ottoman-Polish campaigning. Slow filling-in
lifted the sub-region to 2,45M in 1500, 2,85M in 1600 and 3,25M in 1700
(12,41 inhabitants per km²). Then came an extraordinary
eighteenth-century breakout: between 1700 and 1800, population leaped by
2,6M (+80%) to 5,85M (22,34 inhabitants per km²), advancing another 1M
to 6,85M (26,16 inhabitants per km²) by 1830 — a cumulative 3,51-fold
increase that raised the sub-region’s share of the entire Balkan domain
from 24,9% in 1400 to 34,4% in 1830.

Three converging forces drove this Danubian-Carpathian surge. First,
Transylvania’s integration into the Habsburg Monarchy after 1699 brought
administrative pacification, mining investment and sustained population
expansion among Romanian, Hungarian and Saxon communities — anchored at
1,75M in the 1820 HistoGIS enumeration [\[7\]](#ref-HistoGIS2022).
Second, maize adoption and the decline of Tatar raiding after Russia
annexed Crimea (1783) and Bessarabia (1812) opened the fertile chernozem
plains of Bărăgan, Oltenia and eastern Moldavia to permanent plough
cultivation [\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[5\]](#ref-Ploscaru2022Union).
Third, urban growth pivoted decisively from mediaeval princely seats
toward commercial Danubian capitals: Târgovişte declined from 25k in
1400 to 4k in 1700 and 6,6k in 1830, while Bukarest climbed from 2k in
1400 and 8k in 1600 to 45k in 1700, 56k in 1750 and 77,6k in 1830. In
Moldavia, Iaşi grew from 2k in 1400 to 20k in 1700 and 30,9k in 1830;
the Danubian fortress port of Izmail reached 30k across 1750-1800; and
Chişinău (Kisinev) jumped from 7k in 1800 to 25,2k in 1830 after
becoming the administrative capital of Russian Bessarabia
[\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021).

Finally, Eastern Thrace or Turkey-in-Europe (22.970,2 km², 4 catalogued
settlements) formed a demographic world apart. Occupying barely 2,8% of
the chapter’s land area, it quadrupled from 320k in 1400 (13,93
inhabitants per km²) to 580k in 1500, 880k in 1600, 950k in 1700, 1,15M
in 1800 and 1,28M in 1830 — reaching 55,72 inhabitants per km², more
than double the next densest sub-region. Its trajectory mirrored the
imperial fortunes of its two great metropolises. Constantinople, reduced
to roughly 80k inhabitants by 1400 inside its vast Theodosian walls,
expanded under Ottoman imperial rebuilding to 200k in 1500, 660k in
1550, 680k in 1600 and a pre-industrial peak of 700k in 1700 — making it
the largest city in Europe and the Mediterranean world — before
retreating slightly to 570k in 1800 amid provincial fiscal strain and
rebounding to 690,7k by 1830
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021)[\[11\]](#ref-Gokce2018Iznik).
Upstream on the Maritsa, Edirne surged from 15k in 1400 to 127k in 1500
and peaked at 150k in 1600 when sultans frequently held court there
before stabilising at 100k through 1700-1800 and 90,7k in 1830
[\[8\]](#ref-Sener2019Structural).

# Limitations

Five methodological limitations define the uncertainty bounds of this
reconstruction.

First, Ottoman fiscal records were never designed as modern vital
censuses. Sixteenth-century *tahrir* rolls enumerate taxpaying adult
male heads of households (*hane*) rather than individuals, making
aggregate headcounts sensitive to the household multiplier (4,5 vs 5,2
persons per *hane*) and to the unrecorded share of tax-exempt *askeri*
cadres, religious personnel, garrison troops and transhumant
pastoralists [\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983). After 1600, the transition from
real households to composite *avarız-hane* tax units widens estimation
margins to roughly $\pm 12\%$ to $\pm 15\%$ across interior Ottoman
*sancaks* until the 1828-1831 *nüfus* surveys and post-independence
national censuses restore tighter control
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[9\]](#ref-Mutaf2026Quantifying).

Second, the 635-settlement gazetteer combines two analytically distinct
source classes. While the 148 cities drawn from Eltjo Buringh’s urban
database [\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021) capture genuine historical rises,
sieges and contractions across 1350-1830, the remaining 487 entries
inherited from modern national statistical registries (chiefly Slovenia,
Romania, Greece, Bulgaria and Serbia) function as constant-value
longitudinal municipal backfill anchors. As a result, certain modern
administrative entries visible in the raw peak-size rankings — notably
Bucharest Sector 3 (84,8k), Cluj-Napoca (63,1k), Zemun (34,2k), Larisa
(32,3k) and Čačak (25,8k) — reflect flat backfilled census baselines
rather than fourteenth-century Ottoman *tahrir* tallies; in this
chapter, historical urban dynamics are therefore interpreted strictly
from the 148 longitudinal Buringh urban series while regional aggregates
are anchored to independent fiscal, cadastral and HGIS totals.

Third, spatial settlement recovery is strongly skewed by modern national
statistical archiving rather than early-modern urban density. Romania
(161), Greece (134) and Slovenia (133) together account for 67,4% of the
635 catalogued locales, whereas core Ottoman territories such as Bosnia
and Herzegovina (2), Albania (12), North Macedonia (5), Kosovo (4) and
Montenegro (1) remain thinly represented in digitised municipal series
despite supporting substantial Ottoman *kasaba* networks. Open archival
digitisation initiatives across the Western Balkans
[\[10\]](#ref-Ozates2024Dependency)[\[19\]](#ref-Dakic2025Citizen) are
gradually narrowing this gap.

Fourth, imperial metropolitan and provincial boundaries cut across
modern statistical masks. Istanbul’s 680k to 700k population spanned
both European Stamboul-Galata and Asian Üsküdar on the Bosphorus;
following standard urban historiography
[\[1\]](#ref-Todorov1983)[\[6\]](#ref-Buringh2021), the whole
metropolitan unit is assigned to Eastern Thrace rather than split
arbitrarily across the strait. Similarly, the 1820 Habsburg Crownlands
HGIS benchmark [\[7\]](#ref-HistoGIS2022) covers the entire Austrian
Empire (15 crownlands across 585.303 km²), requiring careful matching of
Transylvania, Croatia, Slavonia, Dalmatia and Illyria against our Balkan
polygons while treating Bohemia, Moravia, Galicia and Lombardy-Venetia
purely as comparative external benchmarks.

Fifth, violent wartime demographic troughs — especially the Great
Turkish War (1683-1699), the Serbian uprisings (1804-1815) and the Greek
War of Independence (1821-1829) — involved simultaneous combat
mortality, epidemic disease and cross-border flight. Point estimates at
1700 and 1830 therefore capture net surviving residents within each
fixed polygon rather than isolating mortality from permanent emigration
into Hungary, southern Russia or the Ottoman Anatolian provinces.

# Conclusion

Across the 480 years between 1350 and 1830, the Ottoman Balkans and
South-Eastern Europe expanded from a plague-depleted, politically
fragmented peninsula of 7,82M inhabitants in 1400 to a populous,
agriculturally resurgent macro-region of 19,91M inhabitants in 1830.
Mean population density climbed 2,55-fold from 9,66 to 24,59 inhabitants
per km², tracing a distinct three-act rhythm: classical Ottoman
pacification and urban reconstruction during the fifteenth and sixteenth
centuries; seventeenth-century climatic, fiscal and wartime stagnation
that actively depopulated Serbia, Kosovo and Greece; and a powerful
eighteenth-century agrarian surge driven by maize cultivation, frontier
quarantine and the clearance of the Lower Danubian plains.

That long demographic cycle reshaped the human geography of
South-Eastern Europe permanently. Constantinople’s post-1453 revival
created Europe’s largest early-modern metropolis and lifted Eastern
Thrace above 55 inhabitants per km², yet the deepest structural shift
unfolded north of the Balkan Mountains and the Sava. By 1830, Wallachia,
Moldavia and Transylvania had grown 3,51-fold to 6,85M — more than a
third of the entire macro-region — and cross-checked cleanly against the
1820 Habsburg Crownlands historical GIS benchmark. Still overwhelmingly
rural and lacking railways or industrial cities on the eve of the 1830s
[\[2\]](#ref-Palairet1997)[\[16\]](#ref-Emini2022Efforts), South-Eastern
Europe nonetheless possessed the dense peasant base, emerging national
capitals and frontier agrarian surpluses that would power its rapid
nineteenth-century demographic transition.
