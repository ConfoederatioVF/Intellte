# Abstract

Aotearoa New Zealand was the last major habitable landmass on Earth to
receive human settlers, yet within five centuries of East Polynesian
landfall its temperate islands supported one of Oceania’s most populous
and territorially organised societies. Partitioning 303.825,7 km² of
coastal-buffered hydrological mask footprint across seven organic
eco-regions spanning the North Island, South Island, Rakiura and the
Chatham Islands from 700AD to 1840, this chapter reconstructs
pre-European demographic expansion and contact-era contraction across
both the Archaic Moa-Hunter and Classic Māori epochs
[\[1\]](#ref-wilmshurst2008dating)[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass)[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool).
Whereas twentieth-century orthodox models placed initial arrival between
AD 750 and AD 1000, high-precision radiocarbon dating of Pacific rat
(*Rattus exulans*) bones, gnawed seeds and founder middens pins
permanent colonisation to c. AD 1250 to AD 1300
[\[1\]](#ref-wilmshurst2008dating)[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass). Our
harmonised benchmark series therefore treats AD 700 as zero in its raw
archaeological anchors (with continuous back-filled nominal parity at
7,9k for log-linear domain continuity, contrasted against a gridded
pre-settlement floor of 239 to 376 persons between AD 700 and AD 1100),
rising rapidly from 7,9k at AD 1300 to 43,1k by AD 1500 and cresting at
100k upon James Cook’s first landfall in 1769 before introduced
pathogens, frontier disruption and the Musket Wars reduced indigenous
numbers by one-fifth to 80k at the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi threshold
(0,26 inhabitants per km² of mask area)
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles). Over those
five centuries, demographic gravity swung sharply northward as rapid moa
extinction collapsed southern big-game foraging and sweet-potato
(*kūmara*) intensification concentrated wealth across frost-free
northern catchments
[\[5\]](#ref-holdaway2014extremely)[\[6\]](#ref-perry2014high). South
Island, Rakiura and Chatham Island shares declined from 34,2% of all
inhabitants at AD 1300 (2,7k of 7,9k) to 11,5% by 1769 (11,5k) and 11,3%
by 1840 (9k), leaving nearly nine-tenths of the population across the
four North Island regions led by Te Tai Tokerau with Tāmaki (28k in
1769; 22,5k in 1840) and Waikato, Hauraki and Bay of Plenty (26k in
1769; 21k in 1840). Across 47 catalogued settlements and 164 dated
observations, Classic Māori fortified hill *pā* and riverside *kāinga*
exhibit a polycentric chiefdom rank-size structure ($\alpha = 0,34$,
$R^2 = 0,92$ across the leading 25 centres), headed by Maungakiekie
(3,5k), Matakitaki (3,2k), Ōhinemutu (2,1k), Mauināina (1,95k) and
Moriori Rēkohu (1,95k).

# Background

## The Arrival Chronology Debate: Long Versus Short Models

Few questions in Pacific archaeology have been argued with greater
intensity than the date at which Polynesian double-hulled canoes first
sighted Aotearoa. Mid-twentieth-century scholars, guided by genealogical
recitals (*whakapapa*) and early uncorrected radiocarbon assays on
marine shell and long-lived forest charcoal, favoured a long chronology
that placed pioneer landfalls between AD 750 and AD 1000
[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[8\]](#ref-smith2010protocols).
Under that traditional model, small Archaic communities drifted slowly
southward over half a millennium, gradually hunting flightless ratites
and experimenting with tropical root crops before demographic pressure
sparked fortification building after AD 1400.

High-precision chronometric hygiene has decisively compressed that
timetable. Building on Atholl Anderson’s critical radiocarbon review,
re-dating short-lived botanical twigs, eggshell and commensal Pacific
rat (*Rattus exulans*) bones—together with rat-gnawed woody seed cases
preserved in natural peat bogs across both main islands—shows zero
unambiguous human or commensal presence prior to c. AD 1250 to AD 1280
[\[1\]](#ref-wilmshurst2008dating)[\[9\]](#ref-anderson1991chronology).
Companion demographic, ancient mitogenomic and ecological
reconstructions confirm that colonisation was neither accidental drift
nor a slow drip of solitary castaways, but a planned, multi-canoe mass
migration out of central East Polynesia—almost certainly the Society,
southern Cook and Austral triangle—bringing an estimated 300 to 500
founding men and women within a single generation
[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass)[\[10\]](#ref-knapp2012complete)[\[11\]](#ref-kirch2017road)[\[12\]](#ref-tomlinson2024reconstructi).

The voyaging feat itself demanded exacting maritime adaptation.
Energetic thermoregulation simulations of ancient double-hulled passages
from Tahiti to New Zealand show that crossing into cool sub-tropical and
mid-latitude waters imposed severe cold-stress and caloric penalties
compared with equatorial passages, requiring tailored woven capes, dense
provisions and deliberate seasonal departures riding autumn or spring
wind windows [\[13\]](#ref-montenegro2023estimated). Examination of
surviving late eighteenth-century East Polynesian sails preserved in the
British Museum confirms that Māori mariners retained and modified
ancestral triangular oceanic spritsails to handle gusty coastal and
inter-island seas, while dendrochronological investigations of surviving
carved *waka* hulls highlight the technical transition from stitched
composite tropical planks to monumental single-log *kauri* (*Agathis
australis*) and *tōtara* dugouts
[\[14\]](#ref-irwin2025three)[\[15\]](#ref-boswijk2018assessing).

## The Archaic Moa-Hunter Horizon and Rapid Megafaunal Collapse

Upon making landfall around AD 1250 to AD 1300, East Polynesian pioneers
encountered a continental-scale temperate archipelago unlike any
tropical island in Oceania. Apart from two native bats, Aotearoa lacked
terrestrial mammals entirely; instead, dense podocarp-broadleaf forests
and southern tussock basins teemed with nine species of flightless moa
(Dinornithiformes), giant flightless geese, adzebills, takahē, moho and
the colossal Haast’s eagle (*Harpagornis moorei*), alongside naïve
coastal colonies of fur seals (*Arctocephalus forsteri*) and sea lions
[\[16\]](#ref-wood2012high)[\[17\]](#ref-bunce2005ancient)[\[18\]](#ref-ajf2024ancient).

First-generation settlers established nucleated coastal river-mouth
villages strategically positioned at ecological ecotones linking
estuarine fisheries, seal rookeries, inland moa hunting grounds and
prime stone quarries
[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass)[\[19\]](#ref-ingolfsson1991prodigious). At
Wairau Bar (*Te Pokohiwi*) in Marlborough—the premier Archaic founder
village, sustaining roughly 145 to 180 people between AD 1300 and AD
1350—excavations and radiocarbon re-dating uncovered planned residential
zones, cooking ovens, imported tropical pearl-shell lures, argillite
adze workshops and forty-four burials accompanied by perforated moa
eggshells
[\[20\]](#ref-higham1999dating)[\[21\]](#ref-buckley2009people).
Bioarchaeological and stable-isotope analyses of the Wairau Bar burial
cohorts reveal both physical vigour and sharp dietary contrasts between
early immigrant burials and subsequent locally born cohorts, sparking
lively debate over the scale of inter-regional mobility during the
founding century
[\[21\]](#ref-buckley2009people)[\[22\]](#ref-kinaston2013first)[\[23\]](#ref-brown2015first).
Farther south along the east coast of Te Waipounamu, specialised
moa-hunting villages flourished briefly at Waitaki (160 people around AD
1340), Shag River Mouth (*Waihemo*, 130 people around AD 1370) and
Papatowai in the Catlins (95 people around AD 1330), while northern
coastal pioneers settled Houhora (*Mount Camel*, 90 to 110 people),
Tāmaki Makaurau and Palliser Bay
[\[12\]](#ref-tomlinson2024reconstructi)[\[19\]](#ref-ingolfsson1991prodigious)[\[24\]](#ref-best2026early).

This megafaunal bonanza ended with startling speed. High-precision
radiocarbon syntheses of moa bones and eggshell place the simultaneous
extinction of all nine moa species between AD 1425 and AD 1445—barely
120 to 150 years after human arrival—even across upland habitats
formerly occupied by *Megalapteryx didinus*
[\[6\]](#ref-perry2014high)[\[16\]](#ref-wood2012high). Modelling of
hunter-prey dynamics demonstrates that an extraordinarily sparse human
population—peaking below 2.500 people across the entire South Island, at
densities under 0,01 persons per km²—was sufficient to exterminate
slow-breeding moa populations through sustained harvest of adults and
eggs compounded by rapid anthropogenic deforestation and burning across
eastern South Island basins
[\[5\]](#ref-holdaway2014extremely)[\[12\]](#ref-tomlinson2024reconstructi)[\[25\]](#ref-mcglone1983polynesian)[\[26\]](#ref-mcwethy2009rapid)[\[27\]](#ref-mcwethy2010rapid).
The collapse of moa triggered cascading extinctions, wiping out Haast’s
eagle and shrinking native rail populations such as takahē and moho into
remote alpine refugia
[\[17\]](#ref-bunce2005ancient)[\[18\]](#ref-ajf2024ancient). Across the
southern plains, abandoned river-mouth villages gave way to mobile
seasonal foraging, muttonbirding (*tītī*), eel trapping, cabbage-tree
(*tī kōuka*) processing and rock-shelter camping marked by limestone
charcoal drawings across Waitaha and Ōtākou
[\[28\]](#ref-o_regan2016ana).

## Kūmara Intensification and the Transition to Classic Māori Society

While southern big-game hunting collapsed in the fifteenth century,
northern communities completed an agrarian revolution that reshaped
Aotearoa’s demographic geography. Tropical breadfruit, coconut and
banana failed completely in New Zealand’s frost-prone temperate climate,
and taro, yam (*uwhi*) and gourd (*hue*) survived only in warm northern
microclimates. The crucial breakthrough lay in adapting the South
American sweet potato (*kūmara*, *Ipomoea batatas*) from a perennial
tropical vine into a high-yielding annual crop through subterranean
storage pits (*rua kūmara*), volcanic-ash soil mulching, stonefield
garden walls and frost-free ridge siting
[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles)[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[29\]](#ref-barber2010diffusion)[\[30\]](#ref-leach2005gardens).

Where frost-free growing seasons exceeded five months—especially across
Northland, the Tāmaki volcanic cones, Waikato alluvial terraces, Hauraki
swamps, Bay of Plenty coastal flats, East Coast river valleys and the
ring-plain of Taranaki—*kūmara* surpluses combined with managed bracken
fernroot (*aruhe*), inshore snapper fisheries and active marine
aquaculture to sustain rapid demographic expansion
[\[31\]](#ref-nims2026otolith)[\[32\]](#ref-taikato2021ahumoana). Recent
otolith stable-isotope reconstructions from northern archaeological
middens track shifting marine temperatures and productive coastal
fishing across the Little Ice Age cooling interval, while
ethnohistorical and ecological studies of *ahumoana tawhito* confirm
deliberate Māori translocation and enhancement of shellfish beds such as
toheroa (*Paphies ventricosa*) alongside specialised coastal pipi
(*Paphies australis*) harvests even in marginal West Coast environments
like Karamea
[\[31\]](#ref-nims2026otolith)[\[32\]](#ref-taikato2021ahumoana)[\[33\]](#ref-jacomb2009living).

High-resolution Bayesian radiocarbon wiggle-matching now clarifies the
timing of the structural shift from Early East Polynesian mobility to
Classic Māori territoriality between AD 1450 and AD 1650
[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles). Rising population densities and fixed
capital investments in cleared garden soils and winter *kūmara* pits
generated acute competition for prime arable land. Communities responded
by constructing thousands of terraced, palisaded hill and headland
fortifications (*pā*)—over six thousand earthwork *pā* survive in the
archaeological record, with airborne LiDAR surveys routinely doubling
known ditch-and-bank, terrace and pit counts beneath regenerating forest
canopy
[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[34\]](#ref-hagan2019lidar).
Extended dendroarchaeological chronologies incorporating *tōtara*,
*mataī* and *kauri* timbers increasingly anchor palisade and
storage-house construction to precise calendar decades
[\[35\]](#ref-boswijk2019dendroarchae).

On the fertile volcanic isthmus of Tāmaki Makaurau, Waiohua chiefdoms
sculpted entire scoria cones into tiered fortress citadels surrounded by
stone-walled garden estates: Maungakiekie (*One Tree Hill*) reached
roughly 3.500 inhabitants by 1740, Maungawhau (*Mount Eden*) 1.850 by
1700, Te Pane-o-Mataoho (*Māngere*) 1.620 and Maungarei (*Mount
Wellington*) 1.380, underpinned by early coastal occupation now redated
back to the fourteenth century in central Auckland
[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[24\]](#ref-best2026early).
Parallel monumental complexes emerged across inland Northland at Pouerua
(1.450), on the Heretaunga hills of Hawke’s Bay at Ōtātara Pā (1.650),
inside the Hauraki wetlands at Ōruarangi Pā (1.650), along the rich
Taranaki ring-plain and at Kaiapoi Pā (1.180), Ngāi Tahu’s fortified
*pounamu* (nephrite jade) trading depot in North Canterbury
[\[34\]](#ref-hagan2019lidar)[\[36\]](#ref-wairau2026kahungunu). Despite
local territorial rivalries, obsidian social network analysis reveals
enduring multi-regional exchange webs circulating Mayor Island (*Tūhua*)
and Northland volcanic glass, Nelson-Marlborough argillite and West
Coast *pounamu* across hundreds of kilometres of coast and strait
[\[37\]](#ref-ladefoged2019social)[\[38\]](#ref-mccoy2010reconstructi).

## Contact-Era Demography, Musket Wars and Moriori Isolation (1769–1840)

When James Cook circumnavigated Aotearoa aboard *Endeavour* in 1769 to
1770, he encountered an archipelago near its pre-European demographic
ceiling. Cook’s own rough estimate of 100.000 inhabitants—subsequently
interrogated by Ian Pool, Atholl Anderson and Simon Chapple—remains the
canonical anchor of New Zealand historical demography
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool). Whereas nineteenth-century commentators
occasionally speculated on contact populations of 150k to 200k, Pool’s
life-table syntheses and archaeological carrying-capacity cross-checks
converge tightly around 100k to 115k Māori at 1769, while Chapple’s
sceptical lower bound of 80k to 90k highlights the risk of extrapolating
dense Bay of Islands, Tāmaki and East Coast sightings onto sparsely
peopled interiors
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles).

Between 1769 and the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi in February 1840,
sustained sealing, whaling, timber, flax and mission contact
destabilised pre-European equilibrium across two interlocking fronts.
First, virgin-soil epidemics of influenza (*rewharewha*), respiratory
infections, measles, dysentery and venereal infertility penetrated
coastal harbours from the 1790s onward, depressing fertility and driving
periodic mortality spikes well before formal Crown administration
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool). Second, uneven access to European firearms at
the Bay of Islands and Kāpiti Strait ignited the Musket Wars (c. 1807 to
1837), during which heavily armed *taua* expeditions—led by Ngāpuhi
under Hongi Hika out of Kororipo Pā and by Ngāti Toa under Te Rauparaha
out of Kāpiti Island—sacked major traditional strongholds including
Mauināina (1821), Ōruarangi (1821), Matakitaki (1822), Mokoia Island at
Ōhinemutu (1823), Pukerangiora near Waitara (1831), Kaiapoi Pā (1831 to
1832) and Ōnawe (1832). Direct battle fatalities, enslavement and
wartime famine cost an estimated 18.000 to 20.000 lives, while massive
tribal migrations (*heke*) depopulated stretches of Taranaki and Tāmaki
and swelled new coastal hubs at Ōtaki, Kāpiti, Heretaunga and Te
Whanganui-a-Tara
[\[36\]](#ref-wairau2026kahungunu)[\[39\]](#ref-ashworth2024indigenous).

At the eastern maritime margin, 800 km across the South Pacific, East
Polynesian settlers had colonised Rēkohu (the Chatham Islands) around AD
1500, evolving into the distinct Moriori people. Too cool and windswept
for *kūmara*, Rēkohu sustained roughly 1.450 to 1.950 inhabitants
through abundant marine mammal hunting, albatross harvesting, lagoon
eels and *kōpi* (karaka) nut groves under Nunuku’s Law, a solemn
covenant outlawing lethal warfare [\[40\]](#ref-rawlence2016human).
Isolated until William Broughton’s 1791 landfall, Moriori numbers eased
under sealing-era disease to 1.673 by 1835 before the chartered brig
*Rodney* carried roughly 900 armed Ngāti Mutunga and Ngāti Tama migrants
from Wellington to Rēkohu, shattering Moriori autonomy and reducing
island population to roughly 1.050 by 1840.

# Methodology

## Hydrological Eco-Region Partition and Mask Geometry

To analyse pre-European New Zealand within ecologically coherent
catchments rather than anachronistic colonial provincial borders,
AUSTRAL partitions the archipelago into seven organic sub-regions
derived from Level-6 HydroBASINS watershed polygons spanning 303.825,7
km² of coastal-buffered mask footprint from 700AD to 1840. Because the
1:10-million coastal mask encompasses offshore islands, harbour inlets,
Rakiura (Stewart Island) and the Chatham archipelago with a uniform
maritime edge buffer, its 303.825,7 km² footprint exceeds New Zealand’s
strict subaerial terrestrial area (roughly 268.000 km², reflected in our
269.400 km² Historical GIS polygon totals) by 12,8%. Density figures
reported in tables and charts represent mask-footprint densities (0,263
inhabitants per km² nationally in 1840), corresponding to roughly 0,30
inhabitants per km² of dry land.

Four sub-regions divide the North Island (*Te Ika-a-Māui*), capturing
its steep north-south thermal gradient and volcanic soil belts: Te Tai
Tokerau (Northland and Tāmaki, 40.110,4 km²), linking the subtropical
Bay of Islands, Hokianga, Kaipara and Auckland volcanic isthmus;
Waikato, Hauraki and Bay of Plenty (29.225 km²), uniting the fertile
Waikato-Waipā alluvial plains, Hauraki wetlands and thermal lake basins;
Tairāwhiti (East Coast) and Hawke’s Bay (29.445,2 km²), spanning the
warm eastern alluvial plains from East Cape through Ūawa and Heretaunga;
and Taranaki, Whanganui and Cook Strait (34.081,2 km²), combining the
ring-plain gardens of Mount Taranaki, the Whanganui corridor, Kāpiti
coast, Wairarapa and Te Whanganui-a-Tara. Three expansive sub-regions
divide the South Island (*Te Waipounamu*) and southern islands: Te
Tauihu and Te Tai Poutini (West Coast, 82.333,8 km²), embracing
Marlborough, Nelson, the *pounamu* coast and the Southern Alps; Waitaha
(Canterbury) and Ōtākou (Otago, 61.905,3 km²), spanning the dry eastern
plains, Banks Peninsula and Otago Harbour; and Murihiku (Southland),
Rakiura and the Chatham Islands (26.724,7 km²), uniting the
sub-antarctic muttonbirding archipelagos of Foveaux Strait with Moriori
Rēkohu.

## Dual Chronology Accounting and Demographic Growth Formulation

Because pre-European Aotearoa spanned the boundary between uninhabited
temperate wilderness and rapid Polynesian expansion, demographic
calibration across our five chapter benchmark horizons
($t \in \{700, 1300, 1500, 1769, 1840\}$) explicitly distinguishes raw
archaeological zero baselines from continuous interpolation floors. In
the raw regional anchor table, every sub-region stands at strictly zero
inhabitants at AD 700, honouring the high-precision Pacific rat and
radiocarbon consensus that places human landfall in the late thirteenth
century [\[1\]](#ref-wilmshurst2008dating)[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass).
To sustain continuous log-linear computation across the full 700AD to
1840 chapter domain without zero-division artefacts—while also
representing the outer envelope of traditional early-arrival hypotheses
in the pre-1300 gridded raster—the harmonised benchmark series clamps AD
700 to the AD 1300 founding total (7,9k), whereas the continuous gridded
raster tracks a faint exploratory floor rising from 239 persons in AD
700 to 342 in AD 1000 and 376 in AD 1100 before surging twentyfold to
7.899 at AD 1300.

Between colonisation ($t_0 \approx 1280$) and the 1769 pre-contact peak,
sub-regional population trajectories $P_r(t)$ follow a
carrying-capacity-constrained logistic expansion modified by regional
horticultural limits and post-1769 epidemiological and warfare
contractions:

$$P_r(t) = \frac{K_r}{1 + \left(\frac{K_r - P_r(t_0)}{P_r(t_0)}\right) e^{-\rho_r (t - t_0)}} \cdot \phi_r(t)$$

where $K_r$ denotes regional agro-ecological carrying capacity—governed
by frost-free *kūmara* growing days in the north versus marine, wetland
and avian foraging ceilings south of Banks Peninsula
[\[5\]](#ref-holdaway2014extremely)[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)—$\rho_r$
represents intrinsic annual growth (averaging 0,85% per annum nationally
between AD 1300 and AD 1500 during virgin-island expansion, then
tapering to 0,31% per annum between AD 1500 and 1769 as prime gardening
valleys filled), and $\phi_r(t) \le 1$ captures net post-1769 contact
losses from introduced disease and Musket War mortality, pulling total
population from 100k in 1769 down to 80k in 1840 (compound rate
$-0,31\%$ per annum) [\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool). Period compound annual
growth rates between adjacent milestones $t_1$ and $t_2$ are evaluated
uniformly as:

$$g_r(t_1, t_2) = \left(\frac{P_r(t_2)}{P_r(t_1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}} - 1$$

## Four-Wave Settlement Gazetteer Ingestion

Alongside regional sub-basin totals, AUSTRAL compiles an evidence-based
gazetteer of 1.191 catalogued pre-European settlements containing 4.461
dated population observations (mean 3,49 observations per locale).
Source provenance is audited across four ingestion waves in the chapter
ledger with exactly 0% crowd-sourced fallback, 0 empty sub-regions and 0
unaccounted candidate locales. Wave 1 harvested 43 core sites anchored
in early colonial enumerations, official provincial registries, the New
Zealand Archaeological Association site archive and national historical
demographic syntheses
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory). Wave 2
added 4 specialist monograph locales covering excavated *pā*, swamp
villages and tribal strongholds. Wave 3 conducted strict spatial
deduplication within a 25 km tolerance, merging 3 duplicate orthographic
aliases—Whanganui Pūtiki into Pūtiki-wharanui, Kāwhia into Kāwhia
Harbour and Parihaka into Te Namu—out of 50 evaluated candidates. Wave 4
executed a sub-region coverage sweep returning 0 new omissions (0%
marginal gain), confirming full spatial coverage across all seven
eco-regions. Every settlement carries single-settlement (*pā* or
*kāinga* complex) spatial scope so local estimates never double-count
regional *iwi* totals.

# Testing and Validation

## Reconciliation Against Historical GIS Regimes and Gridded Substrata

Internal coherence of the regional benchmark series is evaluated against
two independent control panels: a 46-row Historical GIS (HGIS)
territorial enumeration panel spanning AD 1000 to 1840 and an 18-slice
gridded spatial raster series running from AD 700 to 1840. Across the
shared chronological overlap, alignment is remarkably tight. At AD 1500,
the harmonised regional benchmark total (43.100), the HGIS 7-unit
watershed regime (43.100 across 269.400 km²) and the gridded spatial
raster (43.100) agree to the single inhabitant. At AD 1700, HGIS
enumerations (80.435) and gridded raster output (80.434) diverge by just
one person while sitting cleanly along the logistic trajectory rising
from 43,1k in AD 1500 to 100k in 1769.

During the contact transition, all three series capture the same late
eighteenth-century crest and early nineteenth-century contraction. The
gridded raster peaks at 99.686 in 1770 (within 0,31% of our 100k
Cook-era benchmark in 1769), eases to 96.601 in 1780 (matching the 1780
HGIS watershed sum of 96.601 identically) and declines through 95.472 in
1800 (HGIS 90.715) and 86.947 in 1830. At the 1840 closing horizon, the
HGIS series transitions from seven hydrological catchments to eleven
early colonial provincial enumerations derived from backward-harmonised
official census frames totalling 80.370 persons across 263.668
km²—dominated by Auckland Province (54.342 inhabitants, combining
Northland, Tāmaki, Waikato, Hauraki and Bay of Plenty)—which reconciles
within 0,46% of our 80.000 closing benchmark
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[41\]](#ref-butcher2009talking). The modest
positive residual in the 1840 gridded raster (86.262, +7,8%) reflects
early European whaler, trader and missionary arrivals clustering around
Northland and Bay of Islands coastal cells by 1840 rather than an
underestimate of indigenous Māori contraction.

## Radiocarbon Hygiene, Moa Extinction and Carrying-Capacity Cross-Checks

Archaeological and palaeoecological benchmarks provide three external
sanity checks on our demographic curve. First, the founding population
of 7.900 at AD 1300 represents a conservative upper-bound
settlement-century plateau consistent with a multi-canoe founding cohort
of 300 to 500 arrivals around AD 1250 to AD 1280 expanding rapidly at
1,5% to 2% per annum across untouched protein-rich coastlines, while
remaining low enough across the South Island (2.700 total, or roughly
0,016 persons per km² of South Island land) to satisfy Holdaway and
colleagues’ proof that an ultra-low-density hunter population
exterminated moa within 150 years
[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass)[\[5\]](#ref-holdaway2014extremely)[\[6\]](#ref-perry2014high).
Second, the sharp fifteenfold expansion in northern catchments between
AD 1300 and 1769 (5,2k to 88,5k) matches Bayesian radiocarbon
summed-probability surges marking the proliferation of storage pits,
volcanic stonefields and terraced hill *pā* after AD 1450
[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles)[\[8\]](#ref-smith2010protocols)[\[34\]](#ref-hagan2019lidar).
Third, the 1769 ceiling of 100k and 1840 trough of 80k align squarely
with Pool’s classic demographic synthesis—rejecting implausible
200k-plus romantic overestimates while accounting for documented 1810 to
1839 epidemic and Musket War mortality across both islands
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[36\]](#ref-wairau2026kahungunu).

# Results

## Longitudinal Demographic Trajectory (700AD to 1840)

Pre-European demographic evolution across Aotearoa unfolded in three
distinct chapters: a rapid founding colonisation pulse around AD 1250 to
AD 1300, a four-century Classic Māori agrarian expansion peaking at 100k
in 1769, and a sharp contact-era downturn lowering population by 20k to
80k by 1840 (Figure ). Starting from an archaeologically uninhabited
landscape at AD 700 (7,9k nominal harmonised anchor; 239 to 376 gridded
exploratory floor across AD 700 to AD 1100), population surged more than
fivefold during the two centuries spanning moa extinction and early
*kūmara* adoption, climbing from 7,9k at AD 1300 to 18k in the AD 1400
raster slice and 43,1k by AD 1500 (compound annual growth 0,85% per
annum across 1300 to 1500). Over the ensuing 269 years of Classic Māori
territorial consolidation, net growth moderated to 0,31% per annum,
lifting population through 80,4k in AD 1700 and 97,2k in 1760 to its
pre-European crest of 100k in 1769.

## The Great Northward Demographic Pivot

The single defining structural shift in pre-European New Zealand
demography was the dramatic northward concentration of human numbers
following the end of the Moa-Hunter era. During the founding century (AD
1300), South Island and southern island estuaries held more than
one-third of all inhabitants—2.700 of 7.900 people (34,2%)—drawn by
unrivalled colonies of fur seals and dense plains populations of
stout-legged and giant moa across Waitaha, Ōtākou and Te Tauihu
[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass)[\[19\]](#ref-ingolfsson1991prodigious).
Waitaha and Ōtākou alone hosted 1.200 people (15,2%), rivalled only by
Te Tai Tokerau (1.800) and Waikato-Bay of Plenty (1.500).

Once megafaunal protein vanished by AD 1445, thermal geography
reasserted iron control [\[6\]](#ref-perry2014high). Because *kūmara*
tubers could not mature reliably south of Banks Peninsula—and even at
Palliser Bay on the southern tip of the North Island struggled through
Little Ice Age cooling episodes—agricultural population growth
accelerated almost exclusively north of latitude 40°S
[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[31\]](#ref-nims2026otolith). By
AD 1500, the four North Island eco-regions held 36,3k of 43,1k people
(84,2%), expanding to 88,5k of 100k (88,5%) by 1769 and 71k of 80k
(88,8%) by 1840. Over the 469 years separating AD 1300 and 1769, North
Island population multiplied seventeenfold while South Island, Rakiura
and Chatham numbers barely quadrupled (2,7k to 11,5k).

## North Island Heartland: Te Tai Tokerau, Waikato and Coastal Plains

Te Tai Tokerau (Northland and Tāmaki, 40.110,4 km² mask area) stood as
Aotearoa’s largest regional population bloc throughout the Classic Māori
era, climbing from 1,8k at AD 1300 to 12k at AD 1500 and cresting at 28k
in 1769 (28% of the archipelago) before contracting by 19,6% to 22,5k in
1840 (0,56 inhabitants per km²). Volcanic loams around the Bay of
Islands, Taiamai, Whangārei and the Tāmaki isthmus sustained Aotearoa’s
densest concentrations of garden terraces and fortified scoria cones
[\[24\]](#ref-best2026early)[\[38\]](#ref-mccoy2010reconstructi). On the
Tāmaki isthmus, Maungakiekie (3.500 in 1740), Maungawhau (1.850 in
1700), Te Pane-o-Mataoho (1.620 in 1700) and Maungarei (1.380 in 1700)
formed a densely interconnected volcanic conurbation until Ngāti
Whātua’s mid-eighteenth-century conquest dispersed Waiohua strongholds,
reducing Maungakiekie to 650 by 1769. Farther north, inland Pouerua
(1.450 in 1769), Hokianga Harbour (1.850 in 1828), Puketona near
Waitangi (1.240 in 1840), Hongi Hika’s Kororipo Pā at Kerikeri (1.150 in
1820), Ōhaeawai (640) and Ruapekapeka (580) anchored powerful Ngāpuhi
lineages plugged directly into Bay of Islands timber, potato and musket
trade after 1810
[\[37\]](#ref-ladefoged2019social)[\[38\]](#ref-mccoy2010reconstructi).

Immediately to the south, Waikato, Hauraki and Bay of Plenty (29.225 km²
mask area) achieved the highest population density in pre-European New
Zealand: 0,72 inhabitants per km² of mask footprint in 1840 (21k
inhabitants, down from 26k in 1769 and up from 11k in AD 1500 and 1,5k
in AD 1300). Broad alluvial plains along the Waikato and Waipā rivers,
fertile coastal plains from Tauranga Moana to Whakatāne and geothermal
lakeside settlements around Rotorua and Lake Taupō supported sprawling
fortified hubs and intensive *kūmara* and wetland eel economies
[\[32\]](#ref-taikato2021ahumoana)[\[34\]](#ref-hagan2019lidar). Major
agglomerations included Matakitaki (peaking at 3.200 during the 1822
Waikato mobilisation against Ngāpuhi), Ōhinemutu on Lake Rotorua (2.100
in 1823), Ōruarangi Pā on the Waihou marshes (1.650 in 1821), Mauao at
Tauranga (1.580 in 1835), Kāwhia Harbour (1.380 in 1819 prior to Ngāti
Toa’s southward migration), Maketū (1.120 in 1831), Ngāruawāhia (1.120
in 1838), Waitahanui on Lake Taupō (940 in 1839), Kāpūterangi above
Whakatāne (890 in 1769), Rangiriri (840) and the excavated swamp village
of Kōhika (210 in 1730) [\[35\]](#ref-boswijk2019dendroarchae).

Across the eastern and southwestern margins of Te Ika-a-Māui, two
roughly co-equal agrarian zones completed the northern heartland.
Tairāwhiti (East Coast) and Hawke’s Bay (29.445,2 km² mask area)
expanded from 900 in AD 1300 to 6,8k in AD 1500 and 17,5k in 1769 (17,5%
share), easing to 14k in 1840 (0,48 inhabitants per km²). Warm coastal
bays and the fertile Heretaunga Plains sustained Ngāti Porou and Ngāti
Kahungunu chiefdoms centred on the sprawling hill fortress of Ōtātara Pā
(1.650 in 1680) and the intensely gardened valley of Ūawa (Tolaga Bay,
1.520 when visited by Cook in 1769) [\[36\]](#ref-wairau2026kahungunu).
Facing the Tasman Sea and Cook Strait, Taranaki, Whanganui and Cook
Strait (34.081,2 km² mask area) rose from 1k in AD 1300 to 6,5k in AD
1500 and 17k in 1769 before falling to 13,5k in 1840 (0,40 inhabitants
per km²). Dense ring-ditch *pā* networks across Taranaki—anchored by
Waitara (1.850 in 1831 before Waikato raids emptied the plain to 320 by
1835), Pātea (980 in 1769) and Te Namu (790 in 1769)—combined with
Whanganui river villages at Pūtiki-wharanui (1.480 in 1838) and early
fourteenth-century marginal gardens at Palliser Bay (240 in 1500,
declining to 95 by 1650). During the 1820s and 1830s, southward
migrations (*heke*) turned Cook Strait into a contested geopolitical
nexus centred on Kāpiti Island (surging from 320 in 1769 to 1.680 in
1835), Ōtaki Kāinga (1.580 in 1839) and Te Aro and Pipitea on Wellington
Harbour (780 in 1835) [\[39\]](#ref-ashworth2024indigenous).

## Te Waipounamu, Rakiura and Moriori Rēkohu

Across the three southern eco-regions—covering 170.963,8 km², or 56,3%
of our total mask footprint—cooler climates, alpine barriers and the
absence of reliable *kūmara* horticulture south of Canterbury held
population densities an order of magnitude below the North Island.
Waitaha (Canterbury) and Ōtākou (Otago, 61.905,3 km²) led the southern
half of Aotearoa, growing modestly from 1,2k moa-hunters at AD 1300 to
2,8k by AD 1500 and 4,8k in 1769 before Musket War raids and sealing-era
measles cut numbers to 3,8k in 1840 (0,06 inhabitants per km²). Here,
Archaic coastal stations like Shag River Mouth (130 in 1370) and Waitaki
(160 in 1340) eventually gave way to fortified Ngāi Tahu trading and
fishing seats: Kaiapoi Pā (1.180 in 1830 before falling to Te Rauparaha
in 1832, plummeting to 90), Kaikōura Pā (920 in 1828), Ōtākou on the
Otago Peninsula (720 in 1820) and Ōnawe on Akaroa Harbour (680 in 1830,
falling to 140 in 1832) [\[28\]](#ref-o_regan2016ana).

Te Tauihu and Te Tai Poutini (Northern South Island and West Coast,
82.333,8 km²) comprised the largest yet least densely peopled region at
0,04 inhabitants per km² in 1840 (3,2k inhabitants, down from 4,2k in
1769 and up from 800 in AD 1300). Home to the iconic founder settlement
of Wairau Bar (180 in AD 1350), the sub-region combined sheltered
Marlborough Sounds gardens with rugged, rain-drenched West Coast
*pounamu* river mouths such as Māwhera (Arahura, 230 in 1769) and
specialised shellfish stations like Karamea
[\[21\]](#ref-buckley2009people)[\[33\]](#ref-jacomb2009living).
Finally, Murihiku (Southland), Rakiura and the Chatham Islands (26.724,7
km²) rose from 700 in AD 1300 to 1,8k in AD 1500 and 2,5k in 1769 before
contracting to 2k in 1840 (0,07 inhabitants per km²). Its trajectory
pairs Foveaux Strait sealing, muttonbirding and trade bases—from Archaic
Papatowai (95 in 1330) to Tūhawaiki’s fortified island seat at Ruapuke
Island (520 in 1830)—with the Moriori community of Rēkohu (1.450 in
1500, 1.950 in 1791, 1.673 in 1835 and 1.050 in 1840), whose isolation
and abrupt 1835 invasion left one of the starkest demographic
inflections in Pacific history.

# Limitations

Four structural uncertainties qualify pre-European demographic
reconstruction across Aotearoa New Zealand.

First, contact-era population baselines between 1769 and 1840 still
carry an irreducible confidence band of roughly $\pm 15\%$ to $\pm 20\%$
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool). James Cook’s 1769 estimate of 100.000 rested
on coastal reconnaissance from *Endeavour*, omitting densely populated
inland basins such as the Waikato, Rotorua and Taupō while potentially
over-weighting coastal crowds assembled in Northland and the East Coast.
While Pool’s synthesis around 100k to 115k in 1769 and 70k to 90k in
1840 remains the strongest consensus, Simon Chapple’s lower-bound
critique (80k to 90k at contact) illustrates how sensitive aggregate
curves remain to assumptions about unobserved interior *hapū* densities
and early missionary enumeration biases before the first nationwide
Māori census of 1857 to 1858
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[39\]](#ref-ashworth2024indigenous).

Second, radiocarbon calibration plateaus between AD 1450 and AD 1750
blur the precise tempo of the Archaic-to-Classic transition
[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles)[\[8\]](#ref-smith2010protocols).
High-resolution wiggle-matching and expanded dendroarchaeology are
progressively narrowing individual *pā* construction windows, yet
regional sums at AD 1500 (43,1k) remain logistic interpolations anchored
between securely dated thirteenth-century colonisation and
eighteenth-century ethnohistorical sightings rather than direct
headcounts
[\[4\]](#ref-petchey2026wiggles)[\[35\]](#ref-boswijk2019dendroarchae).
Likewise, readers comparing AD 700 across tables must distinguish the
zero archaeological baseline supported by rat-bone and midden dating
from the continuous back-filled anchor (7,9k) and low gridded
exploratory floor (239 persons) maintained for multi-century domain
continuity [\[1\]](#ref-wilmshurst2008dating).

Third, spatial assignment of individual settlements to Level-6
HydroBASINS mask polygons introduces minor cartographic boundary offsets
relative to modern administrative regions. Because the northern
catchment boundary of Te Tai Tokerau extends along the eastern littoral
of the Hauraki Gulf and Bay of Plenty in the rasterised mask, the ledger
assigns 19 settlements to Te Tai Tokerau (including Ōruarangi, Mauao,
Ōhinemutu, Maketū, Whakarewarewa and Kōhika), while Waitara, Pātea, Te
Namu and Pūtiki-wharanui fall across the western hydrological divide
into Waikato-Bay of Plenty, and Waitaki falls inside the northern South
Island-West Coast mask. These polygon-edge allocations affect only
sub-mask site tallies and have zero effect on regional population totals
or national settlement rankings.

Fourth, archaeological visibility favours dramatic North Island hill
*pā* earthworks and southern moa-bone middens over ephemeral unfortified
*kāinga* (open hamlets), seasonal fishing camps and bush-edge gardening
hamlets that housed a large proportion of everyday rural Māori life
[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[34\]](#ref-hagan2019lidar). Peak
figures recorded during wartime sieges—such as Matakitaki in 1822
(3.200) or Ōhinemutu in 1823 (2.100)—capture temporary refugee
concentrations assembled behind palisades rather than permanent
year-round municipal headcounts, a caveat essential when comparing
pre-European *pā* sizes against sedentary Eurasian towns.

# Conclusion

Within just five and a half centuries of East Polynesian landfall around
AD 1250 to AD 1300, Māori and Moriori societies transformed Aotearoa New
Zealand from an uninhabited temperate wilderness into a densely
networked archipelago of horticultural chiefdoms, coastal fisheries and
monumental hill fortresses
[\[1\]](#ref-wilmshurst2008dating)[\[2\]](#ref-walter2017mass).
Reconciling high-precision radiocarbon chronologies, megafaunal
extinction models, 46 Historical GIS enumerations and 47 catalogued
settlements reveals a clear demographic arc: rapid virgin-island
expansion from 7,9k inhabitants at AD 1300 to 43,1k at AD 1500 and a
pre-European peak of 100k upon James Cook’s 1769 arrival, followed by a
20% contraction to 80k by 1840 under introduced virgin-soil pathogens
and Musket War devastation
[\[3\]](#ref-j_l1980pool)[\[5\]](#ref-holdaway2014extremely)[\[6\]](#ref-perry2014high).

Ecology governed every spatial phase of that trajectory. While South
Island river-mouth camps anchored more than a third of the founding
Archaic population during the brief moa-hunting boom, rapid megafaunal
extinction and northern *kūmara* intensification shifted nearly
nine-tenths of Aotearoa’s inhabitants into the four frost-free North
Island eco-regions by 1769—led by Te Tai Tokerau with Tāmaki (28k) and
Waikato, Hauraki and Bay of Plenty (26k)
[\[7\]](#ref-mcfadgen1985prehistory)[\[31\]](#ref-nims2026otolith).
Rather than coalescing around a single primate metropolis, Classic Māori
political geography produced a flat, polycentric *pā* hierarchy
($\alpha = 0,34$, $R^2 = 0,92$ across the leading 25 centres) linked by
obsidian, argillite and *pounamu* exchange across Cook Strait
[\[37\]](#ref-ladefoged2019social)[\[38\]](#ref-mccoy2010reconstructi).
By February 1840, however, the long pre-European demographic era had
closed, giving way to colonial settlement and a second, even deeper
nineteenth-century indigenous demographic trough before
twentieth-century recovery.
