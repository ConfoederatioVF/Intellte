# Abstract

Between the founding of Santafé de Bogotá on the Muisca plateau in 1538
and the dissolution of Gran Colombia in 1830, the northern Andean and
Caribbean jurisdictions that formed the Viceroyalty of New Granada, the
Real Audiencia de Quito and the Captaincy General of Venezuela passed
through a catastrophic sixteenth-century demographic collapse, a century
of uneven mestizaje and agrarian recovery, and a violent independence
transition. Across eight sub-regions covering 2.710.090,9 km² (2,71M
km²), aggregate population fell by 71,7% from an estimated 4,48M
(4.480.000) in 1538 to 1,37M in 1600 and a nadir of 1,27M (1.268.000) in
1650 under epidemic disease, tributary disruption and forced labour in
highland farms and alluvial gold placers
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia)[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean).
Recovery began around 1700 at 1,41M and accelerated across the
eighteenth century to 1,76M (1.763.000) at the 1778 Bourbon census
horizon and 2,42M (2.418.000) on the eve of the 1810 revolutions, before
slowing during the Wars of Independence to 2,79M (2.794.000) in 1830
[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia)[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial)[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe).
Over these three centuries, demographic weight shifted away from the
early indigenous heartlands of Quito’s central highlands and the
Casanare-Caguán missions toward the mestizo artisan corridors of
Santander and Boyacá, the gold-and-cattle frontier of Antioquia and
Cauca, and the cacao-exporting valleys of Caracas and Maracaibo
[\[7\]](#ref-minchom2019people)[\[8\]](#ref-twinam1982miners)[\[9\]](#ref-buschges1995crisis).
Regional trajectories are calibrated against the 1778 Censo de Carlos
III, the 1779-1781 Villalengua enumeration of Quito, Obispo Mariano
Martí’s 1771-1784 pastoral visitation, José de Castro y Aráoz’s 1787
Caracas summary and 1.024 geocoded colonial settlements.

# Background

## Conquest Collapse and Early Colonial Heartland Geography

Spanish entry into the northern Andes was swift, fragmented and
epidemiologically devastating. Within three decades of coastal landings
at Santa Marta (1525), Coro (1527) and Cartagena (1533), expeditions
under Sebastián de Belalcázar, Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada and Nikolaus
Federmann converged upon Quito (1534) and the Muisca plateau of Santafé
and Tunja (1538). Unlike the unified Inca core in Peru, the northern
territories combined dense chiefdoms in the Cundinamarca-Boyacá
highlands and the northern Quito sierra with dispersed riverine villages
along the Magdalena, Sinú, Cauca and Orinoco basins
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean).
Smallpox, measles, influenza and typhus travelled ahead of and alongside
marching columns, striking populations with no prior immunity while
early encomienda tribute, porterage and gold panning fractured
subsistence cultivation
[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia)[\[10\]](#ref-castanogallego2026dominio).

Between 1538 and 1650, the entire domain lost roughly three-quarters of
its inhabitants, falling from 4,48M to 1,27M. The coastal lowlands
suffered the steepest initial destruction: Gobernación de Guayaquil and
Esmeraldas dropped by 83,6% (from 220k to 36k), Antioquia, Popayán and
Chocó by 79,8% (from 620k to 125k), and Cartagena, Santa Marta and
Riohacha by 76,7% (from 580k to 135k)
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[11\]](#ref-obyrneh2013desabastecimiento).
Malaria and yellow fever imported through Atlantic slave ships
compounded indigenous mortality along humid ports and river valleys,
leaving tropical lowlands chronically short of labour
[\[12\]](#ref-uribe2007sociedad)[\[13\]](#ref-gomez2020comida). Highland
basins retained higher survival shares, yet even the densely settled
Muisca heartland of Santafé, Tunja, Pamplona, Mariquita and Neiva fell
by 75,2% from 1,15M in 1538 to 285k in 1650, while the Real Audiencia de
Quito declined by 65,8% from 950k to 325k
[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean)[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia).

## Resguardos, Mestizaje and Mining Frontiers in New Granada

To arrest indigenous depopulation and secure tribute and grain supplies
for urban centres, royal officials established bounded communal lands
(*resguardos*) across Santafé, Tunja and Pamplona at the close of the
sixteenth century
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[14\]](#ref-pitapico2022agregaciones).
In practice, however, demographic decline inside the *resguardos*
continued alongside steady white and mestizo encroachment. By the
mid-eighteenth century, royal visitors ordered the aggregation and
extinction of shrunk *resguardos* across northeastern New Granada,
consolidating surviving tributaries into fewer parishes while opening
former communal pastures to smallholding mestizo farmers and artisans
[\[14\]](#ref-pitapico2022agregaciones)[\[15\]](#ref-galvisnoyes1982restablecimiento).
Nowhere was this social transformation sharper than in Socorro, San Gil
and Vélez, where a dense free peasantry cultivating cotton, tobacco and
sugar cane turned the eastern cordillera into New Granada’s
fastest-growing interior province
[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia)[\[15\]](#ref-galvisnoyes1982restablecimiento).

West of the Magdalena, demography followed gold and enslaved African
labour. After early vein mines at Buriticá and Mariquita faded, placer
extraction shifted into the humid valleys of Antioquia, the Upper Cauca
and the Pacific Chocó
[\[8\]](#ref-twinam1982miners)[\[10\]](#ref-castanogallego2026dominio).
Cartagena de Indias served for two centuries as the primary *asiento*
port of entry for enslaved Africans destined for New Granada and Quito,
developing a large urban *pardo* and *moreno* artisan population
alongside fortified port militias
[\[16\]](#ref-solanod2012artilleros)[\[17\]](#ref-solanod2016pedro)[\[18\]](#ref-bejaranomartinez2026racial).
Inland, slave gangs (*cuadrillas*) worked alluvial terraces along the
Atrato, San Juan and Cauca rivers, supplied with plantains, maize and
jerked beef from Valle del Cauca haciendas
[\[12\]](#ref-uribe2007sociedad)[\[13\]](#ref-gomez2020comida). In
Antioquia, by contrast, small-scale family *mazamorrero* prospecting,
frontier agrarian colonisation and high rates of manumission fostered
rapid natural increase after 1700, lifting Antioquia, Popayán and Chocó
from 125k in 1650 to 265k in 1810
[\[8\]](#ref-twinam1982miners)[\[10\]](#ref-castanogallego2026dominio).

## Obrajes, Coastal Cacao and the Quito-Caracas Divergence

South of Popayán, the Real Audiencia de Quito followed an inverted
economic trajectory. During the seventeenth century, while Potosí silver
sustained Andean commerce, Quito’s northern and central sierra (Otavalo,
Latacunga, Ambato, Riobamba and Quito itself) prospered as Spanish South
America’s premier woollen textile (*obraje*) workshop, drawing large
numbers of *forastero* indigenous migrants escaping *mita* drafts
elsewhere
[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean)[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia)[\[19\]](#ref-sanztapia2003venta).
The eighteenth century broke that prosperity. Cheaper European woollens
entering via Cape Horn and Buenos Aires undercut highland cloth,
volcanic eruptions and earthquakes (notably the 1797 Riobamba
catastrophe) shattered towns and irrigation works, and epidemic
mortality repeatedly struck crowded barrios
[\[7\]](#ref-minchom2019people)[\[9\]](#ref-buschges1995crisis)[\[20\]](#ref-roderaalonso2004relacion).
Consequently, the Quito sierra stagnated, rising only from 325k in 1650
to 375k in 1778, while population tilted southward toward Cuenca’s
cotton-weaving and cascarilla districts and westward to the emerging
cacao export port of Guayaquil
[\[21\]](#ref-simard1997formacion)[\[22\]](#ref-borrero2023mujeres)[\[23\]](#ref-hamerly2022quito).

At the northeastern extremity of the viceroyalty, the Captaincy General
of Venezuela (created as an autonomous captaincy in 1777) experienced
the fastest sustained colonial expansion in northern South America.
Seventeenth-century Caracas planters built fortunes on cacao estates
along the Tuy, Aragua and Barlovento valleys worked by enslaved Africans
and *canario* immigrants, later catalysed by the monopoly Basque
Guipuzcoana Company (1728-1785) and the 1778 free-trade reforms
[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial)[\[24\]](#ref-langue1994desterrar).
Provincia de Caracas and Maracaibo expanded more than threefold from
195k in 1650 to 605k in 1810, overtaking the Quito sierra in total
headcount and turning Caracas into a bustling commercial and
administrative capital marked by sharp stratification between
*mantuanos*, *pardos* and rural poor
[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial)[\[16\]](#ref-solanod2012artilleros)[\[24\]](#ref-langue1994desterrar).

## Bourbon Reform, Revolt and the Independence Demographic Shock

Administrative centralisation under the Bourbon Crown (which permanently
restored the Viceroyalty of New Granada in 1739 and installed the
Intendancy of Caracas in 1776) produced the region’s first systematic
enumerations: the 1778 Censo de Carlos III in New Granada, the 1779-1781
Villalengua census in Quito and Obispo Martí’s 1771-1784 pastoral
visitation in Venezuela
[\[7\]](#ref-minchom2019people)[\[15\]](#ref-galvisnoyes1982restablecimiento).
Yet fiscal tightening, tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, and new sales
taxes provoked immediate resistance. Indigenous communities across the
Quito sierra rose repeatedly in tax and census rebellions during the
1760s and 1770s
[\[25\]](#ref-cornelio2023simbolismo)[\[26\]](#ref-buschges1997leyes),
while in 1781 the Comunero insurrection swept Socorro, Tunja and the
Llanos before royal columns restored order
[\[14\]](#ref-pitapico2022agregaciones)[\[15\]](#ref-galvisnoyes1982restablecimiento).

Worse disruption arrived after 1810. Unlike Peru, which remained a
royalist bastion until 1824, Venezuela and New Granada endured fifteen
years of seesaw war, earthquakes (most destructively the 1812 Caracas
and Mérida shock) and naval blockades
[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe)[\[23\]](#ref-hamerly2022quito).
Pablo Morillo’s 1815-1816 siege of Cartagena, royalist campaigns across
the Magdalena and Venezuelan llanos, and Patriots’ forced marches across
the Andes inflicted heavy military and civilian mortality, above all in
Caracas, Barinas, Cumaná and Cartagena
[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe)[\[11\]](#ref-obyrneh2013desabastecimiento).
Whereas the Santafé-Tunja highlands and Antioquia resumed brisk growth
after Boyacá (1819), Caracas and Maracaibo barely crept upward from 605k
in 1810 to 620k in 1830, registering the deepest independence
demographic scar in northern South America
[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial)[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe).

# Methodology

## Sub-Regional Partition of the Viceroyalty Domain

To capture ecological, economic and jurisdictional contrasts without
projecting modern republican borders backward onto colonial
administration, the 2.710.090,9 km² study domain is divided into eight
colonial sub-regions aligned with Bourbon audiencias, provinces and
mission frontiers. Four sub-regions cover New Granada proper: the
central and eastern cordilleran core of Santafé, Tunja, Pamplona,
Mariquita and Neiva (156.314 km²); the Caribbean littoral of Cartagena,
Santa Marta and Riohacha (158.283,7 km²); the western mining and Cauca
corridor of Antioquia, Popayán and Chocó (231.440,3 km²); and the vast
Orinoco-Amazonian plains of Los Llanos de Casanare and the Caguán
missions (660.351,9 km²). Two sub-regions span the Real Audiencia de
Quito: the Andean sierra together with Jaén, Quijos and Maynas
(482.585,2 km²), and the Pacific lowlands of Gobernación de Guayaquil
and Esmeraldas (74.754,6 km²). The remaining two sub-regions encompass
the Captaincy General of Venezuela: Provincia de Caracas and Maracaibo
(370.168,6 km²), and eastern Cumaná (Nueva Andalucía), Guayana and
Margarita (576.192,6 km²).

This eight-part spatial partition preserves direct comparability between
sixteenth-century encomienda-visita districts, late-colonial Bourbon
*padrones* and early republican departmental censuses. Note that Panama,
though attached to the Viceroyalty of New Granada administratively after
1739, belongs to the Central American and Isthmian regional study and is
excluded from the eight South American sub-regions analysed here,
whereas Maynas and Jaén remain grouped inside the Quito sierra polygon
per colonial audiencia boundaries prior to the 1802 royal cédula
transfer.

## Benchmark Reconstruction and Enumeration Calibration

Population headcounts $P_r(t)$ are reconstructed at seven historical
benchmarks $t \in \{1538, 1600, 1650, 1700, 1778, 1810, 1830\}$,
anchoring the conquest baseline, the sixteenth-century epidemic plunge,
the mid-seventeenth-century nadir, the early Bourbon turn, the Censo de
Carlos III horizon, the eve of independence and the breakup of Gran
Colombia. For each sub-region $r$ of area $A_r$, regional totals obey
the accounting identity:

$$P_r(t) = \sum_{j \in r} P_{j,\text{enum}}(t) \cdot \kappa_j(t) + U_r(t)$$

where $P_{j,\text{enum}}(t)$ denotes enumerated inhabitants in district
$j$, $\kappa_j(t) \in [1,04, 1,22]$ adjusts for documented fiscal
under-registration of dispersed rural families, fugitive tributaries,
uncounted *forasteros* and frontier mission populations, and $U_r(t)$
represents unenumerated autonomous indigenous groups in the Llanos,
Darién, Guajira, Esmeraldas and upper Amazon
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean)[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia).

Between benchmarks, compound annual growth rates follow standard
geometric compounding:

$$g_r(t_1, t_2) = \left(\frac{P_r(t_2)}{P_r(t_1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}} - 1$$

For the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, trajectories rely on
encomienda tribute ratios converted at 3,8 to 4,5 persons per tributary,
ecclesiastical *visitas* and *obraje* labour registers
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean)[\[19\]](#ref-sanztapia2003venta).
For the late colonial baseline around 1778-1787, regional sums lock
directly onto surviving viceregal *estados generales*, adjusted upward
where contemporary fiscal lists omitted outlying mission
*corregimientos* or coastal parishes
[\[7\]](#ref-minchom2019people)[\[15\]](#ref-galvisnoyes1982restablecimiento).
For 1810 and 1830, trajectories reconcile Alexander von Humboldt’s 1800
provincial tabulations, local parish counts during the First Republic
and Gran Colombia’s 1825-1827 departmental enumerations, accounting
explicitly for wartime losses between 1812 and 1822
[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe)[\[23\]](#ref-hamerly2022quito).

## Gazetteer Integration and Settlement Scaling

Parallel to macro-regional headcounts, this chapter indexes 1.024
geocoded colonial settlements across Colombia (558), Venezuela (367),
Ecuador (69) and Peru (30, representing colonial Jaén and Maynas).
Ingestion proceeded through four structured audit waves documented in
the chapter source ledger, drawing 966 settlements (94,34%) from HGIS de
las Indias (1701-1808), 27 primary urban series (2,64%) from Tovar
Pinzón and colleagues’ 1778 New Granada census compilation, 12 highland
and coastal Quito series (1,17%) from Hamerly and Tyrer, 11 Venezuelan
series (1,07%) from Obispo Mariano Martí’s 1771-1784 pastoral visitation
and 8 Amazonian mission stations (0,78%) in Maynas. Crowd-sourced
geographic placeholders account for 0% of the register, and spatial
deduplication across 1.047 candidate rows consolidated 23 duplicate
orthographic variants.

Cross-checking implied compound annual growth rates confirms structural
realism across every epoch. During the conquest crash from 1538 to 1600,
annualised decline averaged -1,9% across the domain, led by -2,53% per
year in Guayaquil-Esmeraldas and -2,37% in Antioquia-Popayán-Chocó.
Between 1650 and 1700, recovery remained sluggish at 0,21% per year
overall as Caracas (+0,54% per year) and Cartagena (+0,24%) offset
persistent decline in Los Llanos (-0,54% per year). During the
eighteenth-century expansion from 1700 to 1810, annual growth
accelerated to 0,49% overall, peaking at 0,79% per year in Provincia de
Caracas and Maracaibo and 0,59% in Antioquia-Popayán-Chocó while Quito’s
textile-stricken sierra crawled at 0,17% per year
[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia)[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial)[\[9\]](#ref-buschges1995crisis).
Finally, between 1810 and 1830, wartime disruption compressed overall
annual growth from 0,99% (the 1778-1810 pace) down to 0,72%, with
Caracas-Maracaibo virtually flat at 0,12% per year while sheltered
highland Santafé-Tunja (+1,23%) and Antioquia-Popayán (+1,33%) absorbed
displaced rural households and sustained natural increase
[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe)[\[8\]](#ref-twinam1982miners).

# Results

## Three-Century Demographic Trajectory (1538-1830)

Viewed across three centuries, the northern Andes and Caribbean littoral
experienced a deep U-shaped demographic contraction followed by a
pronounced geographical realignment (Figure ). From 4,48M inhabitants in
1538, total population plunged to 1,37M in 1600 and touched bottom at
1,27M (1.268.000) in 1650, roughly 28,3% of its contact level.
Stabilization around 1,41M in 1700 gave way to sustained
eighteenth-century expansion: 1,76M (1.763.000) in 1778 (+25,4% over
1700), 2,42M (2.418.000) in 1810 (+37,2% in 32 years) and 2,79M
(2.794.000) in 1830, still 37,6% below the pre-conquest 1538 ceiling.

<img src="figures/viceroyalty_new_granada_population_trajectory.png" width="3200" />

More striking than the aggregate curve was the internal rotation between
highlands, coasts and plantation valleys. In 1538, the Muisca highlands
of Santafé-Tunja (25,7% of the domain) and the Quito sierra (21,2%)
together held nearly half the population (46,9%), while
Caracas-Maracaibo represented just 10,3% (460k). By 1650,
Caracas-Maracaibo had risen to 15,4% (195k) while Quito’s share climbed
temporarily to 25,6% (325k) as coastal populations vanished. By 1810,
however, the balance had swung decisively toward New Granada’s eastern
highlands (22,3%, 540k) and Venezuela’s Caracas-Maracaibo core (25%,
605k), reducing the Quito sierra to 17,8% (430k). Following the heavy
independence losses across Venezuela between 1812 and 1821,
Santafé-Tunja regained first place in 1830 at 690k (24,7%, 4,41
inhabitants per km²), ahead of Caracas-Maracaibo at 620k (22,2%, 1,67
per km²) and the Quito sierra at 485k (17,4%, 1,01 per km²).

## New Granada Highlands, Caribbean Ports and Mining Frontiers

Santafé, Tunja, Pamplona, Mariquita and Neiva (156.314 km²) remained the
demographic spine of New Granada throughout the colonial period,
documented by 229 catalogued settlements. After collapsing from 1,15M in
1538 (7,36 inhabitants per km²) to 285k in 1650 (1,82 per km²), the
region recovered modestly to 305k in 1700 and 365k in 1778 before
surging to 540k in 1810 and 690k in 1830 (4,41 per km², more than double
any other sub-region). While indigenous *resguardos* shrank and were
repeatedly consolidated around Tunja and Sogamoso
[\[14\]](#ref-pitapico2022agregaciones), mestizo smallholders filled the
valleys of Socorro, San Gil, Vélez and Pamplona, creating the dense
rural society that powered the 1781 Comunero revolt
[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia)[\[15\]](#ref-galvisnoyes1982restablecimiento).
Bogotá itself grew steadily from 6.845 inhabitants in 1574 to 16.233 in
1778, 21.464 in 1801 and 31.400 by 1825, while Socorro climbed from
2.140 in 1685 to 12.400 by 1810.

Along the Caribbean littoral, Cartagena, Santa Marta and Riohacha
(158.283,7 km², 155 catalogued sites) fell from 580k in 1538 to 135k in
1650, then climbed steadily to 205k in 1778 and 265k in 1810 before
slowing to 290k in 1830 (1,83 inhabitants per km², second in the
domain). Cartagena de Indias anchored the entire Caribbean defence and
slave-trade system, expanding from 4.620 inhabitants in 1574 to 13.690
in 1778 and 17.600 in 1810
[\[17\]](#ref-solanod2016pedro)[\[18\]](#ref-bejaranomartinez2026racial).
Despite its strategic prominence, the province struggled with chronic
agricultural bottlenecks along the lower Magdalena and relied on grain
and livestock shipments from Mompós, the Sinú savannas and the interior
highlands, leaving its rural population exposed during the 1815 royalist
siege
[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe)[\[11\]](#ref-obyrneh2013desabastecimiento).

Further west, Antioquia, Popayán and Chocó (231.440,3 km², 147
catalogued sites) recorded the steepest proportional rebound in New
Granada, multiplying 2,76-fold from 125k in 1650 (0,54 per km²) to 168k
in 1778, 265k in 1810 and 345k in 1830 (1,49 per km²). Gold mining
structured both settlement and racial composition: enslaved Africans
formed the majority in the wet Chocó placers and large Cauca estates
around Cali, Buga and Popayán (14.800 inhabitants by 1810), while
Antioquia’s autonomous smallholders and miners lifted Medellín from
3.120 inhabitants in 1675 to 14.507 in 1778 and 16.800 in 1810
[\[8\]](#ref-twinam1982miners)[\[10\]](#ref-castanogallego2026dominio)[\[13\]](#ref-gomez2020comida).
By contrast, Los Llanos de Casanare and the Caguán missions (660.351,9
km², 26 sites) endured prolonged contraction, slipping from 240k in 1538
to 72k in 1650 and a late-colonial low of 38k in 1778 (0,06 inhabitants
per km²) following the 1767 Jesuit expulsion, recovering only to 54k by
1830 (0,08 per km²).

## Audiencia de Quito: Highland Stagnation and Guayaquil’s Rise

The Real Audiencia de Quito (482.585,2 km², 55 catalogued sites)
exhibits the sharpest contrast between early colonial resilience and
late-colonial stagnation. Falling from 950k in 1538 to 325k in 1650, the
highland core recovered slowly to 355k in 1700 and 375k in 1778 before
crawling to 430k in 1810 and 485k in 1830 (1,01 inhabitants per km²).
Within that modest aggregate, population shifted markedly southward:
while Quito’s urban parishes peaked at 36.479 in 1782 before sliding to
23.701 in 1783 and Riobamba dropped from 11.200 in 1780 to 6.420 after
the 1797 earthquake, Cuenca expanded vigorously from 5.845 in 1574 to
18.919 in 1780 and 29.646 in 1783 as *obraje* decline pushed weavers and
smallholders toward southern valleys
[\[9\]](#ref-buschges1995crisis)[\[21\]](#ref-simard1997formacion)[\[22\]](#ref-borrero2023mujeres).

Simultaneously, the Pacific coastal lowlands of Gobernación de Guayaquil
and Esmeraldas (74.754,6 km², 45 catalogued sites) reversed centuries of
depopulation. After plunging by 83,6% from 220k in 1538 to 36k in 1650,
Guayaquil entered a late-colonial cacao boom after 1765, lifting the
sub-region from 38k in 1700 to 49k in 1778, 72k in 1810 and 95k in 1830
(1,27 inhabitants per km²), while the port city of Guayaquil more than
tripled from 5.214 inhabitants in 1765 to 18.400 by 1825
[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia)[\[23\]](#ref-hamerly2022quito).

## Captaincy General of Venezuela: Cacao Boom and Wartime Scarring

From 460k in 1538, Provincia de Caracas and Maracaibo (370.168,6 km²,
277 catalogued sites) dipped only to 185k in 1600 before rising steadily
through every seventeenth- and eighteenth-century benchmark: 195k in
1650, 255k in 1700, 415k in 1778 and 605k in 1810 (1,63 per km²)
[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial). Urban growth kept pace across both
coast and valleys: Caracas rose from 6.420 inhabitants in 1696 to 19.009
in 1772 and 25.281 in 1805, Maracaibo doubled from 10.312 in 1774 to
22.000 in 1800, Barquisimeto reached 12.602, San Carlos de Austria
12.136, Mérida 11.500, El Tocuyo 10.923, Barinas 10.000 and Valencia
10.000. Adjacent Cumaná (Nueva Andalucía), Guayana and Margarita
(576.192,6 km², 90 catalogued sites) grew in parallel from 95k in 1650
to 148k in 1778 and 195k in 1810, led by Cumaná (16.800 in 1800) and
Barcelona (14.000). Yet Venezuela paid the heaviest price for
emancipation: earthquakes in March 1812, royalist-patriot attrition and
llanero cavalry campaigns stalled Caracas-Maracaibo at 620k in 1830
(+2,5% over twenty years) and held Cumaná-Guayana-Margarita to 215k
(0,37 per km²).

## Urban Hierarchy and Settlement Structure

Across all 1.024 catalogued colonial settlements, peak populations
conform closely to a classic colonial administrative and commercial
hierarchy rather than a single primate capital (Figure ). Because the
Viceroyalty spanned three separate audiencias and captaincies, primacy
remained polycentric: Quito (36.479 in 1782), Bogotá (31.400 in 1825),
Cuenca (29.646 in 1783), Caracas (25.281 in 1805) and Maracaibo (22.000
in 1800) formed a tight upper tier, followed by port and regional
centres between 10k and 20k including Guayaquil (18.400), Cartagena
(17.600), Cumaná (16.800), Medellín (16.800), Popayán (14.800),
Barcelona (14.000) and Socorro (12.400). On a log-log rank-size scale,
the upper tail aligns cleanly with a power-law slope ($\alpha = 0,6$,
$R^2 = 0,98$), reflecting integrated colonial urban networks linked by
mule roads, river navigation on the Magdalena and Orinoco, and coastal
cabotage
[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia)[\[7\]](#ref-minchom2019people)[\[16\]](#ref-solanod2012artilleros).
Below the 26 towns exceeding 10k inhabitants sit 45 towns between 5k and
10k, 432 parishes between 1k and 5k, and 521 rural pueblos and missions
below 1k inhabitants.

# Limitations

Four structural uncertainties govern colonial demographic reconstruction
across northern South America. First, contact-era population sizes in
1538 remain sensitive to pre-conquest carrying-capacity assumptions and
early epidemic timing, particularly in tropical lowlands along the
Magdalena, Sinú and Guayaquil coasts where archival tribute rolls begin
only in the 1550s and 1570s
[\[1\]](#ref-lombardi1975historia)[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia).
Regional uncertainty intervals of roughly $\pm 16\%$ around benchmark
figures reflect this sixteenth-century spread.

Second, fiscal evasion, casta mobility and parish boundary changes
complicate eighteenth-century *padrones*. In New Granada and Quito,
indigenous families reclassified as *mestizos* or *forasteros* dropped
off tribute lists, while enslaved miners in Chocó and runaway *palenque*
settlements escaped regular parish returns
[\[3\]](#ref-stavig1998andean)[\[7\]](#ref-minchom2019people)[\[12\]](#ref-uribe2007sociedad).
Moreover, municipal versus *corregimiento* boundaries frequently overlap
in late-colonial tabulations, explaining why HGIS parish counts for
Caracas after 1800 record urban parish totals around 4k to 6k alongside
full-city totals exceeding 25k, and why Cartagena and Barquisimeto
appear under both municipal and broader jurisdictional series.

Third, archival survival varies sharply across frontiers. While Santafé,
Tunja, Caracas and highland Quito retain dense parish series, Los Llanos
de Casanare, Caguán, Maynas and Guayana experienced severe
administrative fragmentation after the 1767 Jesuit expulsion, forcing
reliance on missionary *relaciones* and fragmentary 1780-1800 reports
[\[14\]](#ref-pitapico2022agregaciones)[\[19\]](#ref-sanztapia2003venta).

Fourth, wartime dislocations between 1810 and 1825 interrupted civil and
ecclesiastical record-keeping precisely when mortality peaked. Gran
Colombia’s 1825 departmental census therefore supplies an uneven
post-war floor, particularly in eastern Venezuela and the upper Orinoco,
requiring back-interpolation across 1810-1830
[\[6\]](#ref-reyescardenas2010derrumbe)[\[23\]](#ref-hamerly2022quito).

# Conclusion

Between 1538 and 1830, the territories of the Viceroyalty of New
Granada, the Real Audiencia de Quito and the Captaincy General of
Venezuela underwent one of the most dramatic demographic inversions in
the Spanish Empire. Stripped of 71,7% of their inhabitants between
contact (4,48M) and 1650 (1,27M), these eight sub-regions recovered
slowly to 1,41M in 1700 before accelerating under Bourbon commercial
expansion, *mestizo* smallholder colonisation, placer mining and cacao
exports to 1,76M in 1778 and 2,42M in 1810. By 1830, despite severe
independence casualties across Venezuela and Cartagena, total population
reached 2,79M (2.794.000) across 2,71M km², anchored by a polycentric
urban network of 1.024 catalogued settlements stretching from Maracaibo
and Caracas through Bogotá and Medellín to Quito, Cuenca and Guayaquil.
Within that arc, demographic leadership had passed decisively from the
*obraje* sierra of Quito to the Cundinamarca-Boyacá cordillera, the
Antioquia-Cauca frontier and the Caribbean valleys of Venezuela
[\[2\]](#ref-kalmanovitz2008economia)[\[4\]](#ref-hamerly1990historia)[\[5\]](#ref-ferry2024colonial).
