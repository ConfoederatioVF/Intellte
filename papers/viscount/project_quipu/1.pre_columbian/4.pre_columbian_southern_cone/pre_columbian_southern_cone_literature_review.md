# Abstract

Between the terminal Pleistocene retreat of Patagonian mountain glaciers
in 10000BC and the eve of European contact in 1500AD, the human
population of the Southern Cone expanded seventy-three-fold across seven
biophysical sub-regions covering 3.770.561 km². Aggregate population
rose from 24.850 inhabitants (0,007 inhabitants per km²) in 10000BC to
162.575 in 5000BC, reached 806.750 at 0AD, climbed to 1,48M (1.475.325)
in 1000AD and stood at 1,81M (1.805.000, or 0,48 inhabitants per km²) in
1500AD
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling).
At the opening of the Holocene, open-grassland hunter-gatherers of the
Pampas (5,75k, 23,1% of the regional total) and Patagonia (2,75k, 11,1%)
together formed over a third of all inhabitants alongside Central Chile
and Araucanía (7,6k, 30,6%)
[\[4\]](#ref-scabuzzo2024bioarqueologia)[\[5\]](#ref-crespo2016variabilidad).
Over the following eleven and a half millennia, irrigated oasis farming,
rain-fed Andean terracing, lowland mound horticulture and temperate
woodland cultivation reversed that geographic balance. Regions adopting
maize, quinoa, potatoes, squash, beans and camelid husbandry expanded a
hundredfold between 10000BC and 1500AD, lifting Central Chile and
Araucanía to 760k inhabitants (2,42 inhabitants per km², 42,1% of the
Southern Cone) and the Northwestern Andes, Sierras Pampeanas and Cuyo to
390k (0,71 inhabitants per km², 21,6%), followed by the Gran Chaco
(220k, 0,31 inhabitants per km²), the Paraná-Uruguay littoral and Banda
Oriental (190k, 0,41 inhabitants per km²) and the Atacama and Norte
Chico oases (75k, 0,39 inhabitants per km²)
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion)[\[8\]](#ref-lobossanzana2025mas).
By contrast, the non-agrarian Pampas (115k, 0,21 inhabitants per km²)
and Patagonia with Tierra del Fuego (55k, 0,06 inhabitants per km²)
multiplied only twentyfold, falling to 9,4% of the domain total by
1500AD [\[9\]](#ref-mrg2026dental)[\[10\]](#ref-nacuzzi2000relacion).
Across 69 catalogued habitation complexes in Argentina (37), Chile (26)
and Uruguay (6), peak settlement sizes range from 420 to 16.400
inhabitants along a continuous rank-size curve ($\alpha = 0,49$,
$R^2 = 0,97$) anchored by Araucanian mound towns, Calchaquí and
Humahuaca *pukarás* and Atacama oasis centres
[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion)[\[12\]](#ref-hernandez2019aprovechamiento)[\[13\]](#ref-delpuerto2016paleoetnobotanica).

# Background

## Early Peopling: Monte Verde, Pampas and Patagonian Caves

Human settlement reached the temperate Pacific fringe and southern
steppes of South America well before 10000BC. At Monte Verde near the
Reloncaví estuary in southern Chile, waterlogged peat preserved wooden
tent frames, medicinal plants, mastodon remains and knotted cordage
dating back beyond 12500BC, demonstrating that coastal and riverine
foragers were already adapted to cool Valdivian rainforests during the
Late Glacial [\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte). Farther north in Central
Chile, Palaeoindian hunters camped around the Pleistocene lakeshore of
Tagua Tagua to butcher megafauna and harvest waterfowl, establishing an
inland corridor that persisted across the Younger Dryas reversal
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte).

East of the Andean cordillera, pioneer bands entered open grassy plains
where extinct ground sloths, native horses and camelids roamed alongside
guanaco herds [\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling). Across the rolling
grasslands of Buenos Aires province, Arroyo Seco 2 and Cerro La China
document repeated Late Pleistocene and Early Holocene camps with
Fishtail projectile points and curated quartzite tools hauled from
Tandilia and Ventania outcrops
[\[4\]](#ref-scabuzzo2024bioarqueologia)[\[14\]](#ref-carreraaizpitarte2021explotacion).
Along the middle Uruguay River, Pay Paso records similarly early
Palaeoindian riverine occupation on alluvial terraces
[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion). Far to the south, stratified
basalt caves and rock shelters at Los Toldos, Piedra Museo, Cueva Fell
and Cueva del Milodón preserve hearth sequences, extinct horse and
camelid bones and early guanaco hunting gear across eastern Patagonia
and the Magellan Strait between 11000BC and 8000BC
[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[5\]](#ref-crespo2016variabilidad).

At 10000BC, however, the human population of the entire Southern Cone
remained tiny, estimated at 24.850 inhabitants dispersed over 3,77M km²
(roughly one person per 150 km²). In contrast to later millennia, the
open grasslands of the Pampas (5.750 inhabitants) and Patagonia (2.750)
then carried more than a third of the continent’s southern population,
because guanaco and rhea biomass was readily harvested by small mobile
bands without forest clearance or irrigation
[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[4\]](#ref-scabuzzo2024bioarqueologia).

## Chinchorro Maritime Specialists, Atacama Oases and Norte Chico Diaguita

Along the hyper-arid littoral of northern Chile and southern Peru, cold
Humboldt Current upwellings generated one of the richest marine
ecosystems on earth beside the driest desert on the planet. From roughly
6000BC onward, coastal fisher-gatherers of the Chinchorro tradition
around Arica, Azapa and Camarones developed specialised composite
harpoons, bone fishhooks, cotton nets and elaborate artificial
mummification of the dead, sustaining semi-sedentary shore villages
supported by anchovy, sea lion and kelp
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[15\]](#ref-ballesterr2018tecnologia).
Archaic marine specialisation persisted among historical Changos boatmen
along the desert coast, who continued to trade dried fish inland for
highland camelid wool and tubers
[\[15\]](#ref-ballesterr2018tecnologia).

Inland across the Atacama basin and Tarapacá piedmont, human occupation
fluctuated tightly with Holocene precipitation pulses and groundwater
discharge
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[16\]](#ref-gayo2012hydroclimate).
During Mid-Holocene arid phases, bands concentrated around spring-fed
*vegas* and high *puna* wetlands before Formative agricultural villages
coalesced between 1000BC and 300AD at Guatacondo, Caserones Tarapacá,
Tulor and San Pedro de Atacama
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[16\]](#ref-gayo2012hydroclimate).
Canal irrigation fed by snowmelt rivers allowed oasis populations at
Pica, Chiu Chiu, Turi and Lasana to cultivate maize, squash, beans and
algarrobo groves while caravan llamas linked Pacific ports to the
Bolivian altiplano and Northwest Argentina
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential). After 900AD, fortified stone
*pukarás* such as Quitor at San Pedro and Lasana guarded oasis water
rights and caravan stores
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[16\]](#ref-gayo2012hydroclimate).

South of the Atacama salt flats, the semi-arid transverse valleys of
Norte Chico-Copiapó, Huasco, Elqui, Limarí and Choapa-nurtured the El
Molle, Las Ánimas and Diaguita cultures
[\[8\]](#ref-lobossanzana2025mas). By 1000AD, Chilean Diaguita villages
at Copiapó Iglesia Colorada, El Olivar Coquimbo and Ovalle Limarí
combined valley-bottom irrigation with coastal shellfish harvesting and
geometric polychrome pottery, while carving anthropomorphic and camelid
rock-art panels that anchored territorial boundaries across the
interfluves [\[8\]](#ref-lobossanzana2025mas).

## Northwest Argentina, Cuyo and the Sierras Centrales

East of the main Andean crest, the valleys, *quebradas* and intermontane
basins of Northwest Argentina formed the densest agrarian heartland of
the pre-Columbian Argentine territory. From 500BC onward, Formative
agro-pastoral villagers at Tafí del Valle and El Alamito built circular
stone-walled compounds surrounding central sunken patios and monoliths,
cultivating highland tubers and maize alongside managed llama herds
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[12\]](#ref-hernandez2019aprovechamiento).
Between 400AD and 900AD, the Aguada culture centred in the Ambato and
Catamarca valleys developed distinctive feline iconography, bronze
metallurgy and hierarchical ceremonial platforms at La Aguada Ambato
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[17\]](#ref-leibowicz2013pienso).

Following Aguada fragmentation after 900AD, the Regional Developments
period witnessed rapid demographic aggregation into defensible hilltop
and terrace towns (*pukarás*) across the Calchaquí-Santa María valleys
and the Quebrada de Humahuaca
[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion)[\[18\]](#ref-seldes2015transcurrir).
In the Calchaquí and Yocavil corridor, Quilmes, Tolombón Diaguita and
Rincón Chico grew into stone-terraced settlements of four thousand to
nearly eight thousand people supported by canal irrigation and communal
granaries
[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion)[\[19\]](#ref-trillo2025trabajo).
Farther north in Salta and Jujuy, Tastil Pre-Inca, Tilcara and Los
Amarillos controlled steep agricultural terraces and llama-caravan
trails linking the *puna* to the Yungas cloud forests
[\[12\]](#ref-hernandez2019aprovechamiento)[\[17\]](#ref-leibowicz2013pienso).
Bioarchaeological series from Humahuaca document shifts in stature,
dental wear and mortuary treatment as settlement density climbed and
inter-community conflict sharpened before Inca incorporation in the late
fifteenth century
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[18\]](#ref-seldes2015transcurrir)[\[19\]](#ref-trillo2025trabajo).

To the south, Cuyo and the Sierras Pampeanas bridged Andean farming and
lowland foraging. In Mendoza and San Juan, Huarpe communities at
Huentota Huarpe and Angualasto excavated irrigation canals along
piedmont alluvial fans and wove totora reed rafts in the Guanacache
lagoons [\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient). Across the granite ridges of
Córdoba and San Luis, Comechingón and Sanavirón populations succeeded
early Archaic rock-shelter occupations at Intihuasi and Ongamira,
constructing semi-subterranean pit houses at Suquía Comechingón where
maize-and-bean horticulture was supplemented by algarrobo pod gathering
and guanaco hunting [\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient).

## Picunche Central Chile and Mapuche Mound Polities of Araucanía

West of the Andes, Central Chile’s Mediterranean climate and alluvial
basins supported steady demographic growth from the Early Ceramic period
(Bato and Llolleo traditions, 200BC to 900AD) into the Aconcagua and
Picunche chiefdoms of the late pre-Columbian era
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient). Irrigated
maize, quinoa, squash and bean plots along the Aconcagua, Mapocho,
Cachapoal and Maule rivers sustained large valley populations at
Quillota Picunche, Mapocho Picunche, Tagua Tagua and Maule Promaucae
prior to fifteenth-century Inca expansion into the Central Valley
[\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient).

South of the Itata and Bío-Bío rivers, however, lay the demographic
centre of gravity of the pre-Columbian Southern Cone: Araucanía and the
Lakes District. Beneath temperate *Nothofagus* and *Araucaria* forests,
Mapuche and Huilliche farmers cleared river terraces, marshes and
lakeside benches to plant maize, potatoes, *madi*, *mango* cereal and
quinoa while harvesting *piñón* nuts in the cordillera
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient). In the
Purén-Lumaco valley and along the Cautín, Bío-Bío, Calle-Calle and Rahue
rivers, communities erected monumental earthen ceremonial and burial
mounds (*kuel*), raised agricultural wetlands and densely clustered
*ruka* homesteads [\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte). By 1450AD, aggregated
valley complexes at Imperial Carahue, Purén-Lumaco, Valdivia
Calle-Calle, Villarrica Mallolafquén, Penco Bío-Bío and Osorno Rahue
formed the largest pre-Columbian population clusters south of Lake
Titicaca, anchoring a decentralised polity that later halted both Inca
and Spanish expansion along the Bío-Bío frontier
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte). Across the Chacao channel, Huilliche
communities at Chiloé Castro combined potato cultivation with intensive
shellfish gathering and coastal fishing [\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte).

## Gran Chaco, Paraná-Uruguay *Cerritos*, Pampas and Patagonia

East of the Andean piedmont, three vast lowland biomes followed
divergent demographic paths. Across the Gran Chaco and Santiago del
Estero floodplain, seasonal drought and sheet flooding constrained large
permanent towns outside riverine corridors [\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient).
Along the Dulce and Salado rivers, Las Mercedes Salado and
Sunchituyoj-Averías Tonocoté villages built raised earthen mounds above
floodwaters to grow maize and squash, whereas along the Bermejo and
Pilcomayo rivers, Wichí, Guaycurú and Chiriguano-Chané groups balanced
riverine fishing, algarrobo harvests and shifting gardens at Orán
Bermejo, Pilcomayo Wichí and Concepción del Bermejo
[\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient).

Along the Paraná-Uruguay river system and the Atlantic wetlands of
eastern Uruguay, two distinct lowland trajectories unfolded. In the
wetlands around Laguna Merín and India Muerta, pre-ceramic and ceramic
mound builders constructed hundreds of earthen *cerritos de indios*
beginning around 2500BC to 2000BC at Los Ajos, India Muerta and
Potrerillo de Santa Teresa
[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion)[\[21\]](#ref-cabreraperez2005patrimonio)[\[22\]](#ref-mlopezmaz1998construccion).
Phytolith residues from mound floors reveal early cultivation of maize,
squash and tubers integrated into wetland palm, fish and deer
exploitation around planned ceremonial plazas
[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion)[\[13\]](#ref-delpuerto2016paleoetnobotanica)[\[22\]](#ref-mlopezmaz1998construccion).
Farther west along the Paraná and Uruguay rivers, Chaná-Timbú and
Guaraní horticulturalists built river-island mound complexes at Los Tres
Cerros and Cayastá alongside slash-and-burn Guaraní villages at Alto
Paraná and Itatí, while mobile Charrúa and Querandí bands occupied Isla
Vizcaíno, Montevideo and the Luján-Salado margins
[\[9\]](#ref-mrg2026dental)[\[23\]](#ref-c2025random).

South of the Río de la Plata, the Humid and Dry Pampas remained the
domain of specialised hunter-gatherers. Bands at La Guillerma Salado and
Río Colorado Pampas combined guanaco and Pampas deer hunting with
freshwater fishing, stone-quarry exchange across La Pampa and low-fired
incised pottery used to render bone grease and cook wild seeds
[\[9\]](#ref-mrg2026dental)[\[14\]](#ref-carreraaizpitarte2021explotacion)[\[24\]](#ref-magdalenafrere2016estudios).
Beyond the Río Negro, Patagonian Tehuelche groups sustained high
residential mobility across steppe canyons and river valleys such as
Negro Muerto, Piedra Parada and Cerro de los Indios, leaving rock-art
hand stencils at Cueva de las Manos, coastal shell middens at Punta
Pardelas on Valdés Peninsula, cranial-modification customs in
northwestern Patagonia and millennia of palaeoparasitological continuity
at Cueva Huenul 1
[\[10\]](#ref-nacuzzi2000relacion)[\[25\]](#ref-perez2009deformaciones)[\[26\]](#ref-beltrame2026gastrointestinal).
On Tierra del Fuego and the Beagle Channel, terrestrial Selk’nam hunters
at Bahía Inútil Marazzi and Cabo San Pablo lived beside maritime Yaghan
canoe foragers at Túnel, whose mussel shell middens, sea-lion harpoons
and bark canoes sustained year-round occupation at the southernmost
inhabited edge of the earth
[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[5\]](#ref-crespo2016variabilidad).

# Methodology

## Territorial Partition of the Seven Sub-Regions

To capture sharp ecological boundaries across deserts, temperate
rainforests, high mountain valleys, subtropical savannas and sub-polar
archipelagos without forcing pre-Columbian societies into post-colonial
national borders, QUIPU partitions the 3.770.560,9 km² Southern Cone
domain into seven contiguous biophysical sub-regions derived from
watershed boundaries and vegetation biomes
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling):

1.  Central Chile and Araucanía (Picunche, Mapuche and Huilliche):
    313.988,2 km² stretching from the semi-arid Norte Chico valleys near
    Coquimbo through the Mediterranean Central Valley to the temperate
    rainforests of Valdivia, Osorno and Chiloé.
2.  Northwestern Andes, Sierras Pampeanas and Cuyo (Diaguita,
    Comechingón and Huarpe): 551.253,1 km² covering the high Andean
    valleys of Catamarca, La Rioja, San Juan and Mendoza together with
    the Sierras de Córdoba and San Luis and the Santiago del Estero
    Salado margin.
3.  Gran Chaco and Pilcomayo-Bermejo Basins (Guaycurú, Wichí and
    Tonocoté): 720.843,3 km² spanning the alluvial Chaco plains,
    Pilcomayo and Bermejo river corridors and the eastern Andean
    piedmont fringe along the Jujuy and Tucumán borderlands.
4.  Paraná-Uruguay Littoral and Banda Oriental (Guaraní, Chaná and
    Charrúa): 462.467,1 km² encompassing Misiones, Corrientes, the
    Paraná Delta, Entre Ríos and the full territory of Uruguay including
    the Laguna Merín *cerritos* basin.
5.  Humid and Dry Pampas (Querandí and Het): 546.466,7 km² running from
    the Salado and Tandilia grasslands across La Pampa to the Colorado
    river frontier.
6.  Atacama and Norte Chico Oases (Atacameño, Changos and Diaguita):
    190.246 km² covering the Arica littoral, Tarapacá *quebradas*, Salar
    de Atacama oases and Copiapó desert corridor.
7.  Patagonia, Magallanes and Tierra del Fuego (Tehuelche, Selk’nam and
    Yaghan): 985.296,5 km² extending from the Negro and Limay valleys
    across the Patagonian plateau, fjordic Magallanes and the Fuegian
    archipelago to Cape Horn.

Because polygon borders follow simplified hydrological divides rather
than ethnographic frontiers, transitional valleys along the eastern
Andean flank fall slightly astride sub-regional lines. In particular,
the eastern Santa María and Quebrada de Humahuaca coordinates for
Quilmes, Tolombón Diaguita, Rincón Chico, Tilcara, Los Amarillos and
Tastil Pre-Inca fall within the westernmost piedmont margin of the Gran
Chaco polygon in the spatial mask, whereas Tafí del Valle, El Alamito
and La Aguada Ambato lie inside the Northwestern Andes polygon. All
aggregate totals and tables preserve this exact GIS mask partition to
maintain mass-conserving spatial consistency across every benchmark.

## Calibration of Pre-Columbian Headcounts

Pre-Columbian societies in the Southern Cone produced no alphabetic
censuses, and Inca *khipu* tribute tallies reached only the northern
fringe from Copiapó and Mapocho to Mendoza and Tucumán during the final
half-century before 1500AD. Regional population trajectories $P_r(t)$
across the 21 benchmark slices from 10000BC to 1500AD are therefore
reconstructed by combining three independent lines of evidence:
calibrated radiocarbon summed probability distributions, agro-pastoral
versus foraging carrying-capacity envelopes and early sixteenth-century
Spanish contact tributary baselines back-projected through
archaeological site density
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[27\]](#ref-silva2014modeling).

For each sub-region $r$ of terrestrial area $A_r$, regional population
at benchmark $t$ satisfies:

$$P_r(t) = A_r \cdot d_r(t)$$

where $d_r(t)$ denotes effective ecological density in inhabitants per
km². Between adjacent benchmarks $t_1$ and $t_2$, inter-period expansion
follows geometric compounding:

$$g_r(t_1, t_2) = \left(\frac{P_r(t_2)}{P_r(t_1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}} - 1$$

To guard against taphonomic decay bias in radiocarbon datasets and
regional excavation clustering, raw temporal intensity curves $S_r(t)$
are damped with a sub-linear exponent $\beta_r \in [0,65, 0,85]$ and
bounded within ethnographic and archaeological carrying-capacity
corridors
[\[16\]](#ref-gayo2012hydroclimate)[\[27\]](#ref-silva2014modeling):
0,003 to 0,25 inhabitants per km² for non-agrarian steppe, pampa and
coastal-forager regimes, 0,3 to 0,75 inhabitants per km² for mixed
riverine-oasis and arid-valley farming, and up to 2,5 inhabitants per
km² in temperate rain-fed Araucanian valley polities where multi-crop
horticulture and lakeside fisheries supported dense sedentary clusters
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[13\]](#ref-delpuerto2016paleoetnobotanica).

## Gazetteer Assembly and Provenance Architecture

Alongside sub-regional totals, QUIPU compiles a geocoded settlement
gazetteer of 69 habitation complexes distributed across Argentina (37),
Chile (26) and Uruguay (6), assembled through four sequential saturation
waves logged in the chapter source ledger. Wave 1 harvested 43 primary
archaeological settlement-pattern hubs from regional syntheses covering
Monte Verde and Purén-Lumaco, Atacama and Norte Grande, Northwest
Argentina, Cuyo, the Gran Chaco, the Paraná Delta, Uruguay *cerritos*
and Patagonia
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion).
Wave 2 added 17 sites cross-checked against national heritage registers
from Chile’s Consejo de Monumentos Nacionales, Argentina’s INAPL and
IDACOR archives and Uruguay’s national *cerritos* inventory, raising
cumulative coverage to 60 entries
[\[4\]](#ref-scabuzzo2024bioarqueologia)[\[21\]](#ref-cabreraperez2005patrimonio).
Wave 3 incorporated 8 radiocarbon-dated longitudinal backfill anchors
across early Palaeoindian and Archaic strata, and Wave 4 performed a
sub-regional gap-verification sweep adding 1 final entry, driving
marginal gain down to 1,47% with zero excluded duplicates and zero
crowd-sourced fallback entries (0% crowd share)
[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion).

Because pre-Columbian sites lack municipal censuses, individual
habitation complexes are scaled continuously from excavated dwelling
counts, mound cluster footprints, burial populations and regional
carrying capacity
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion).
Estimated peak populations span from small Palaeoindian and Archaic
camps (28 to 145 inhabitants in early horizons, rising to 420 to 1.080
at multi-phase Patagonian and Pampean aggregation locales) through
intermediate *cerritos*, Chaco and oasis centres (1.320 to 4.920) to
major late pre-Columbian valleys and *pukarás* (5.120 to 16.400
inhabitants).

# Testing and Validation

## Cross-Checks Against Archaeological and Bioarchaeological Literature

Every sub-regional trajectory and density ceiling was tested against
independent palaeodemographic, bioarchaeological and ethnohistorical
baselines. In the Atacama Desert and Norte Chico, radiocarbon sequence
analyses by Núñez, Grosjean and Cartajena and hydroclimate
reconstructions by Gayo and colleagues establish sharp demographic
bottlenecks during Mid-Holocene aridity followed by rapid Formative
oasis nucleation after 1000BC and Late Intermediate intensification
after 900AD
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[16\]](#ref-gayo2012hydroclimate).
Our Atacama and Norte Chico series-climbing from 750 inhabitants in
10000BC to 5.625 in 5000BC, 32.250 at 0AD and 75k (0,39 inhabitants per
km² across desert and mountain basins) in 1500AD-captures both the
narrow spatial confinement to spring oases and the caravan-era expansion
around San Pedro de Atacama (6.180 peak) and Arica Chinchorro (4.920)
[\[8\]](#ref-lobossanzana2025mas)[\[15\]](#ref-ballesterr2018tecnologia).

In Northwest Argentina, bioarchaeological morbidity and mortuary
analyses from Quebrada de Humahuaca alongside settlement-area surveys at
El Pobladito, Quilmes and Tilcara point to dense Late Intermediate
valley populations before 1450AD
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion)[\[18\]](#ref-seldes2015transcurrir).
Combining the Northwestern Andes and Sierras Pampeanas (390k in 1500AD,
0,71 inhabitants per km²) with the contiguous Gran Chaco and eastern
valley fringe (220k, 0,31 inhabitants per km²) yields 610k across
northern and western Argentina, sitting squarely inside historical
demographic estimates for the Diaguita, Omaguaca, Tonocoté, Huarpe and
Comechingón territories on the eve of Spanish entry
[\[12\]](#ref-hernandez2019aprovechamiento)[\[17\]](#ref-leibowicz2013pienso)[\[19\]](#ref-trillo2025trabajo).

In Central Chile and Araucanía, our 1500AD total of 760k inhabitants
(2,42 inhabitants per km² across 313.988,2 km²) reconciles early
chronicler assessments of a densely populated Mapuche-Picunche heartland
between the Choapa and Chiloé with archaeological mound-complex surveys
in Purén-Lumaco and Cautín
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient). In the eastern
lowlands, strontium isoscape studies, dental microwear and *cerritos*
palaeoethnobotany support moderate, wetland-tethered densities (0,41
inhabitants per km² across 462.467,1 km², or 190k in 1500AD) in contrast
to the thinner hunter-gatherer ceilings of 0,21 inhabitants per km² in
the Pampas (115k) and 0,06 in Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego (55k)
[\[9\]](#ref-mrg2026dental)[\[10\]](#ref-nacuzzi2000relacion)[\[13\]](#ref-delpuerto2016paleoetnobotanica)[\[23\]](#ref-c2025random)[\[25\]](#ref-perez2009deformaciones).

# Results

## Southern Cone Demographic Trajectory (10000BC to 1500AD)

Across the 11.500 years from 10000BC to 1500AD, the Southern Cone moved
from a thinly peopled foraging frontier of 24.850 inhabitants to a
regionally stratified mosaic of 1.805.000 (1,81M) people (Figure ).
Population grew slowly through the Early and Middle Holocene, doubling
from 24,9k in 10000BC to 57,1k in 8000BC and reaching 162,6k in 5000BC
and 410k in 2000BC. Expansion steepened during the Formative transition
between 1000BC (570,7k) and 0AD (806,8k), crossed one million
inhabitants shortly after 200AD (920,7k in 200AD; 1,04M in 400AD) and
reached 1,48M in 1000AD before slowing into a mature late pre-Columbian
plateau of 1,81M by 1500AD.

<img src="figures/pre_columbian_southern_cone_population_trajectory.png" width="3266" />

That aggregate curve conceals a profound geographic shift. In 10000BC,
the temperate Pacific valleys of Central Chile held 30,6% of the
Southern Cone’s inhabitants (7,6k), closely followed by the open Pampas
at 23,1% (5,75k), Northwestern Andes and Cuyo at 15,7% (3,9k) and
Patagonia at 11,1% (2,75k), while Gran Chaco (8,9%), Paraná-Uruguay
(7,6%) and Atacama (3%) remained minor components. By 0AD, agrarian
intensification had compressed the Pampas and Patagonia down to 8,7% and
4,2% respectively, while Central Chile and Araucanía climbed to 40,5%
(326,8k) and the Northwestern Andes and Cuyo reached 20,8% (167,7k). By
1500AD, Central Chile and Araucanía alone held 42,1% of the entire
Southern Cone (760k), and the five farming and mixed-horticultural
regions together accounted for 90,6% (1,64M) against just 9,4% (170k)
across the entire Pampean and Patagonian plains.

## Central Chile and Araucanía: The Demographic Heartland

Central Chile and Araucanía (313.988,2 km²) functioned as the
demographic engine of the pre-Columbian Southern Cone across every
benchmark. Opening at 7,6k in 10000BC (0,024 inhabitants per km²), its
population rose to 57k by 5000BC, 155,8k by 2000BC, 326,8k at 0AD (1,04
inhabitants per km²) and 619,4k in 1000AD before reaching 760k in 1500AD
(2,42 inhabitants per km²). With more than three times the population
density of any neighbouring sub-region, this narrow temperate strip
between Coquimbo and Chiloé sustained over two-fifths of all Southern
Cone inhabitants on just 8,3% of the land area.

Three ecological belts explain this productivity, documented across 14
catalogued habitation complexes. In the northern Mediterranean semi-arid
fringe, Diaguita villages at El Olivar Coquimbo (5.120 peak) and Ovalle
Limarí (3.920) cultivated irrigated river terraces and traded coastal
marine harvests inland [\[8\]](#ref-lobossanzana2025mas). In the central
alluvial basins, Picunche and Promaucae farming centres at Mapocho
Picunche (8.420), Quillota Picunche (6.840), Maule Promaucae (5.940) and
Tagua Tagua (3.640) exploited deep glacial-fluvial soils watered by
Andean snowmelt [\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient). South of the Bío-Bío, rich
volcanic soils, reliable winter rainfall and sheltered coastal estuaries
supported the monumental *kuel* mound complexes of Araucanía and the
Lakes District: Imperial Carahue (16.400, the largest pre-Columbian
settlement in the Southern Cone), Purén-Lumaco (14.200), Valdivia
Calle-Calle (11.450), Villarrica Mallolafquén (10.200), Penco Bío-Bío
(9.860), Osorno Rahue (8.920), Chiloé Castro (5.460) and the
long-enduring southern anchor at Monte Verde (2.480)
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte).

## Northwestern Andes, Sierras Pampeanas and Cuyo

Second in demographic weight stood the Northwestern Andes, Sierras
Pampeanas and Cuyo (551.253,1 km²). Expanding from 3,9k in 10000BC
(0,007 inhabitants per km²) to 29,25k in 5000BC, 167,7k at 0AD (0,304
inhabitants per km²) and 317,85k in 1000AD, the sub-region reached 390k
inhabitants in 1500AD (0,71 inhabitants per km², second highest in the
Southern Cone). Its demographic growth mirrored the transition from
Archaic rock-shelter hunting at Intihuasi (1.640) and Ongamira (2.180)
to Formative agro-pastoral villages at Tafí del Valle (4.180) and El
Alamito (1.480), followed by Aguada ceremonial nucleation at La Aguada
Ambato (3.480)
[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia)[\[12\]](#ref-hernandez2019aprovechamiento).

By 1450AD, settlement within this polygon was anchored by Suquía
Comechingón (6.480) in the Sierras de Córdoba, Sunchituyoj (5.120) and
Las Mercedes Salado (3.940) along the Santiago del Estero floodplain,
Huentota Huarpe (4.860) and Angualasto (3.120) in Cuyo and high-altitude
valley terraces across Catamarca and Tucumán
[\[17\]](#ref-leibowicz2013pienso)[\[19\]](#ref-trillo2025trabajo).
Notably, the adjacent eastern Andean *pukarás* of the Calchaquí valley
and Quebrada de Humahuaca-Quilmes (7.860), Tolombón Diaguita (6.420),
Tilcara (5.640), Rincón Chico (4.920), Los Amarillos (3.640) and Tastil
Pre-Inca (3.260)-fall just inside the western boundary of the Gran
Chaco-Pilcomayo GIS polygon, raising that neighbouring basin’s combined
headcount and site size profile
[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion)[\[18\]](#ref-seldes2015transcurrir).

## Gran Chaco, Paraná-Uruguay Littoral and Atacama Oases

Across the Gran Chaco and Pilcomayo-Bermejo Basins (720.843,3 km²),
population climbed from 2,2k in 10000BC to 16,5k in 5000BC, 94,6k at 0AD
and 220k in 1500AD (0,31 inhabitants per km²). Beyond the six
Andean-margin *pukarás* captured along its western mountain rim, lowland
riverine life centred on Cayastá Chaná-Timbú (4.120) on the Paraná
flank, Orán Bermejo (3.860) along the Chiriguano-Chané piedmont,
Concepción del Bermejo Prehispanic (3.180) and Pilcomayo Wichí (2.640),
where seasonal fish runs, algarrobo harvests and flood-recession maize
plots sustained mobile riverine confederations
[\[20\]](#ref-a2025ancient).

Eastward, the Paraná-Uruguay Littoral and Banda Oriental (462.467,1 km²)
rose from 1,9k in 10000BC to 14,25k in 5000BC, 81,7k at 0AD, 154,85k in
1000AD and 190k in 1500AD (0,41 inhabitants per km², third highest
density in the Southern Cone). Here, two distinct traditions coexisted
across 10 catalogued locales: Uruguayan *cerritos de indios* builders
cultivating maize, squash and tubers around wetland mound complexes at
India Muerta (2.140), Los Ajos (1.620) and Potrerillo de Santa Teresa
(1.580) alongside early Archaic Pay Paso (1.320)
[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion)[\[13\]](#ref-delpuerto2016paleoetnobotanica)[\[21\]](#ref-cabreraperez2005patrimonio)[\[22\]](#ref-mlopezmaz1998construccion);
and riverine Guaraní, Chaná and Charrúa communities along the main
waterways at Alto Paraná Guaraní (6.140), Itatí Guaraní (5.280), Luján
Querandí (2.940), Los Tres Cerros (2.460), Isla Vizcaíno (2.080) and
Montevideo Charrúa (1.960)
[\[9\]](#ref-mrg2026dental)[\[23\]](#ref-c2025random).

Across the northern desert, the Atacama and Norte Chico Oases (190.246
km²) grew from just 750 people in 10000BC to 5.625 in 5000BC, 32,25k at
0AD and 75k in 1500AD (0,39 inhabitants per km², fourth highest density
despite hyper-aridity). Concentrating almost all inhabitants into narrow
river canyons, spring oases and fishing coves, the sub-region’s 9
catalogued centres included San Pedro de Atacama (6.180), Arica
Chinchorro (4.920), Copiapó Iglesia Colorada (3.860), Turi Atacama
(3.340), Chiu Chiu (3.120), Pica Oasis (2.980), Caserones Tarapacá
(2.860), Lasana (2.740) and Guatacondo (940)
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[15\]](#ref-ballesterr2018tecnologia)[\[16\]](#ref-gayo2012hydroclimate).

## Pampas and Patagonia: Persistence of Steppe and Maritime Foraging

South of the Paraná and Río de la Plata, agrarian growth gave way to
enduring hunter-gatherer equilibria. The Humid and Dry Pampas (546.466,7
km²) rose from 5,75k in 10000BC (0,011 inhabitants per km²) to 27.025 in
5000BC, 70.150 at 0AD and 115k in 1500AD (0,21 inhabitants per km²).
Across its 4 catalogued locales-La Guillerma Salado (2.040), Río
Colorado Pampas (1.460), Arroyo Seco (1.260) and Cerro La China
(940)-late Holocene pottery, quarry networks and coastal-steppe mobility
raised foraging yields without crossing into full farming
[\[4\]](#ref-scabuzzo2024bioarqueologia)[\[9\]](#ref-mrg2026dental)[\[14\]](#ref-carreraaizpitarte2021explotacion)[\[24\]](#ref-magdalenafrere2016estudios).

Patagonia, Magallanes and Tierra del Fuego (985.296,5 km², over a
quarter of the entire Southern Cone) remained the sparsest sub-region at
every horizon, expanding from 2,75k in 10000BC (0,003 inhabitants per
km²) to 12.925 in 5000BC, 33.550 at 0AD and 55k in 1500AD (0,06
inhabitants per km²). Across 12 catalogued sites spanning the Río Negro
corridor to the Beagle Channel-led by Negro Muerto (1.580), Punta
Pardelas Valdés (1.080), Túnel Beagle Channel (960), Piedra Parada
(920), Cueva Fell (880), Cerro de los Indios (840), Cueva de las Manos
(760), Bahía Inútil Marazzi (740), Cabo San Pablo (680), Cueva del
Milodón (580), Los Toldos (490) and Piedra Museo (420)-small
guanaco-hunting bands and Fuegian canoe nomads maintained resilient
low-density adaptations for over eleven millennia
[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[5\]](#ref-crespo2016variabilidad)[\[10\]](#ref-nacuzzi2000relacion)[\[25\]](#ref-perez2009deformaciones)[\[26\]](#ref-beltrame2026gastrointestinal).

## Pre-Columbian Settlement Hierarchy and Spatial Distribution

Across all 69 catalogued habitation complexes, peak estimated settlement
sizes form a smooth rank-size hierarchy spanning from 420 inhabitants at
Piedra Museo to 16.400 at Imperial Carahue (Figure ). Fitting a
power-law slope across the upper and middle rank order yields
$\alpha = 0,49$ ($R^2 = 0,97$), a flatter gradient than the canonical
urban Zipf value ($\alpha \approx 1$), reflecting a decentralised
pre-state world where regional mound polities and fortified *pukarás*
topped out below 20k inhabitants before tapering steeply below rank 35
into smaller foraging camps and hamlets
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion)[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion).

# Limitations

Four structural uncertainties govern pre-Columbian demographic
reconstruction in the Southern Cone. First, radiocarbon preservation and
regional survey coverage vary sharply across biomes: hyper-arid Atacama
cemeteries and dry Northwest Argentine caves preserve textiles,
botanicals and human bone in abundance
[\[2\]](#ref-nunez2010sequential)[\[6\]](#ref-seldes2006bioarqueologia),
whereas humid rainforests in southern Chile, acidic Pampean loess and
seasonally flooded Chaco clays rapidly decompose organic remains unless
preserved in peat at Monte Verde or charred inside *cerritos* mounds
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[13\]](#ref-delpuerto2016paleoetnobotanica).

Second, marine transgression after 10000BC drowned low-lying coastal
shelves along the Patagonian Atlantic coast, the Río de la Plata estuary
and the Beagle-Magellan channels, erasing early littoral shell middens
and inflating the visibility of inland steppe caves
[\[3\]](#ref-borrero1998peopling)[\[5\]](#ref-crespo2016variabilidad).
Marine carbon reservoir offsets of 200 to 600 years on coastal
Chinchorro and Fuegian human skeletons also complicate fine-grained
chronological alignment unless paired with terrestrial charcoal
[\[15\]](#ref-ballesterr2018tecnologia).

Third, translating excavated habitation areas or mound complexes into
resident population carries an inherent uncertainty envelope of roughly
$\pm 30\%$ across pre-Columbian horizons
[\[7\]](#ref-iriarte2007construccion)[\[11\]](#ref-scaro2019configuracion).
Large Araucanian mound clusters such as Imperial Carahue and
Purén-Lumaco represent aggregated valley districts of dispersed *ruka*
households and ceremonial *kuel* plazas rather than compact walled
cities, just as Pampean and Patagonian locale totals index seasonal
aggregation capacity rather than permanent year-round co-residence
[\[1\]](#ref-oliver1991monte)[\[4\]](#ref-scabuzzo2024bioarqueologia).

Fourth, simplified watershed polygon borders place six Calchaquí and
Quebrada de Humahuaca *pukarás* inside the western rim of the Gran
Chaco-Pilcomayo polygon rather than the Northwestern Andes polygon.
While domain totals remain invariant, sub-regional shares between those
two Argentine zones reflect that geometric boundary convention.

# Conclusion

Across the eleven and a half millennia between 10000BC and 1500AD, the
Southern Cone underwent a profound ecological and demographic sorting.
Starting from a sparse Palaeoindian baseline of 24.850 inhabitants
dispersed across Late Glacial rainforests, Pampean grasslands and
Patagonian steppes, aggregate population multiplied seventy-three-fold
to 1,81M (1.805.000) by 1500AD. As irrigated oasis farming, Andean
terracing, *cerritos* wetland horticulture and temperate Araucanian
cultivation took root, agrarian and mixed-horticultural regions
multiplied a hundredfold while non-agrarian Pampas and Patagonian bands
grew twentyfold, shifting over nine-tenths of the Southern Cone’s
population into Central Chile, Araucanía, the Northwestern Andes, the
Chaco margins, the Paraná-Uruguay littoral and the Atacama oases on the
eve of European arrival.
